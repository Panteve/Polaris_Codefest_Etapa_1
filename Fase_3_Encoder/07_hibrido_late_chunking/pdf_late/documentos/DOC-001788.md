### Introduction

#### This year marks 25 years since the United Nations Security Council Resolution

#### 1325 on women, peace and security (WPS) stressed the link between gender

#### equality and international peace and security. The resolution underscored the

#### importance of the full and equal participation of women in all efforts towards

the maintenance and promotion of peace and security, including UN peace operations. Following the resolution, organizations and member states involved

#### in multilateral peace operations affirmed their commitment to increase women’s

#### participation in these activities. Increasing the number of uniformed and civilian

#### women at all levels has since become a priority of the UN Secretariat. The

#### Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) Secretariat and,

#### more recently, the European External Action Service (EEAS) of the European

Union (EU) have followed suit and developed strategies establishing specific targets and deadlines to enhance women’s participation in their respective

#### personnel. In 2025, 25 years after the adoption of the resolution, some progress

has been achieved in enhancing women’s representation in multilateral peace operations, but much remains to be done.

This overview of women’s representation in multilateral peace operations presents data on aggregate personnel trends, annual snapshots of data on

#### women’s representation in leadership, and annual averages of women in the

#### personnel of peace operations and in member state contributions. It looks at

#### UN peace operations (i.e. both UN peacekeeping operations and UN special

#### political missions), EU Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP) missions

#### and operations, and OSCE field operations. The objective is to support the

efforts of multilateral organizations and their member states to increase the representation of women in multilateral peace operations.

#### The statistics presented here aim to inform and foster the debate on the future of

#### the women and peace and security agenda within the context of multilateral

peace operations. They illustrate to what extent the organizations deploying multilateral peace operations and their member states are

making progress towards increasing women’s representation in multilateral peace operations. Having a clearer picture of the status

of women’s participation in multilateral peace operations and keeping track of progress also help in recognizing and targeting

#### potential shortcomings. Moreover, this overview enhances the transparency of multilateral peace operations and provides

#### insights into their recent trends and developments.

### Women in the UN military

The UN’s 2018 Uniformed Gender Parity Strategy established the goal of reaching 25 per cent women among military experts and staff officers and 15 per cent among troops in UN peace operations by 2028. Under the Action for Peacekeeping (A4P) Declaration of Shared Commitments, the UN advocated for increasing the number of women in peacekeeping across all levels and key positions. Subsequently, Action for Peacekeeping Plus (A4P+) emphasized the need to enhance the meaningful participation of women in these roles. The proportion of women among military experts and staff officers is nearing the strategy’s target and has surpassed the intermediary 2025 target of 22 per cent. At 8 per cent, however, the proportion of women among troops remains significantly below both the 2025 intermediary target (12 per cent) and the 2028 goal.

#### 13% (+13%)

#### Leadership

On 1 October 2025, 13 per cent of (1 out of 8) force commanders in UN peacekeeping operations were women. This was an increase of 13 percentage points compared to the previous year. The increase can be attributed to the naming of a woman force commander for UNDOF, a position held in the previous year by a man.

#### Totals

While the number and proportion of women serving as troops in UN peace operations increased in 2025, both the number and proportion of women military experts and staff officers decreased. Despite this year’s growth in the proportion of women troops, the pace of increase over the past decade has remained slow. In 2025, the increase was 0.2 percentage points. As of June 2025, women made up 8.8 per cent of the total military personnel deployed in UN peace operations (55 170), an increase of 0.2 percentage points from the previous year. Among military experts and staff officers, women’s representation was substantially higher than for troop positions, at 23 per cent, despite a decrease of 0.4 percentage points in comparison with the previous year.

### Women in the UN police

The UN’s 2018 Uniformed Gender Parity Strategy established the target of 20 per cent women in formed police units and 30 per cent women among individual police officers by 2028. The A4P initiative reaffirmed the commitments of the UN and its member states. In 2021, the UN reached the 30 per cent goal for the proportion of women among individual police officers. In 2025, this proportion was 31 per cent, the same as in the previous two years. The proportion of women in formed police units increased in 2025, surpassing this year’s target of 15 per cent and reaching 18 per cent.

#### 20% (–20%)

#### Leadership

On 1 October 2025, 20 per cent of (1 out of 5) all chief police officers (4 police commissioners and 1 deputy senior police adviser) in UN peace operations were women. The sole woman in police leadership was a police commissioner at UNISFA. For the second consecutive year, this was a decrease of 20 percentage points compared to the previous year. In 2025, the decrease was due to the departure of the woman leading the UN police at UNMISS and the assumption of her role by a man.

#### Totals

The total number of women in the police personnel of UN peace operations increased in 2025 compared to the previous year. This was primarily driven by an increase in the number of women in formed police units, as the number of women among individual police officers decreased slightly. Likewise, the proportion of women increased only within formed police units. Among individual police officers, the proportion of women has remained relatively stable (30–32 per cent) since 2021, when it reached the Uniformed Gender Parity Strategy’s 2028 target.

### Women in UN civilian personnel

The UN’s 2017 System-wide Strategy on Gender Parity set targets for the equal representation of women and men in several areas, including among civilian personnel in UN peace operations. The strategy allows for variation within a 47–53 per cent range as an indication of such parity. In May 2024, the Department of Peace Operations issued a new Gender-responsive UN Peacekeeping Operations Policy. It builds on previous strategies and efforts by reaffirming the need to promote both civilian and uniformed gender parity across all levels and roles, including by creating an enabling environment for the full, equal and meaningful participation of women deployed in peacekeeping operations. However, the goal of achieving gender parity in civilian personnel remains distant for most UN peace operations. In 2025, only UNSCOL had at least 47 per cent women in its civilian personnel.

1 On 1 October 2025, 30 per cent of (7 out of 23) heads of UN peacekeeping operations and special political missions were women. This was the same number and proportion as the previous year. 1This number includes field offices that are not considered multilateral peace operations according to SIPRI’s definition, namely UNOAU, UNOCA, UNOWAS, UNSCO and UNSCOL.

#### Totals

The number of women in the civilian personnel of UN peace operations increased slightly in 2025. In terms of proportion, the variation was less than 1 percentage point compared to the previous year for both peacekeeping operations and special political missions.

### Women in OSCE field operations

The OSCE Secretariat’s Gender Parity Strategy for 2019–26 set the goal of gender parity across all staff categories by 2026, including in OSCE field operations, allowing for variation within a 45–55 per cent range. The OSCE 2025 Annual Progress Report on Gender Equality reconfirmed the strategy and its target and pointed to gains in women’s representation, particularly at senior levels in contracted positions. Nevertheless, the OSCE continued to observe low nomination rates of women to senior positions among its member states. Five out of 12 operations had at least 45 per cent women in their personnel in 2025: the OSCE Centre in Ashgabat, OMIK, OM Moldova, OM Serbia and the OSCE Project Co-ordinator in Uzbekistan.

#### 25% Leadership (+17%)

2 On 1 October 2025, 25 per cent of (3 out of 12) heads of mission of OSCE field operations were women. This was an increase of 17 percentage points compared to the previous year. The increase reflects women assuming leadership, as acting heads, of PO Dushanbe and OM Serbia and the closure of the PRCIO, which had been led by a man. 2 This number includes field operations that are not considered multilateral peace operations according to SIPRI’s definition, namely OM Montenegro, the OSCE Centre in Ashgabat, the OSCE Project Co-ordinator in Uzbekistan, PO Astana, PO Bishkek and PO Dushanbe.

#### Totals

Up until its termination in March 2022, the SMM had been both the largest OSCE field operation and the one with the most women in its deployed personnel. It had shaped both the number and the proportion of women in OSCE operations. Excluding the SMM, the number of women in OSCE field operations has remained relatively constant, with 103 in June 2025. The proportion of women in OSCE field operations other than the SMM had been steady until 2025, when it rose by 7.8 percentage points compared to the previous year and reached 45 per cent, reflecting increases across most operations. This was the largest year-on-year rise in the proportion of women in OSCE field operations over the past decade.

#### Operations

Most OSCE field operations have a relatively low number of personnel. Therefore, small variations in the number of women can have a large impact on the proportion of women in the personnel of a field operation. In 2025, meeting the target of the Secretariat’s Gender Parity Strategy, 5 field operations had at least 45 per cent women (up from 1 field operation in the previous year). OMIK, the largest OSCE operation by number of personnel, had both a relatively high number and a relatively high proportion of women personnel deployed.

#### Contributors

The countries that contribute the highest number of personnel in total to OSCE field operations also tend to contribute the highest number of women personnel. In 2025, Albania, France, Georgia and Serbia were among both the top 10 contributors by number of women personnel and the top 10 contributors by proportion of women personnel to OSCE field operations. Among the top 10 contributors by number of women personnel, women made up 45 per cent or more of the contributions in 6 countries: Albania, France, Georgia, Italy, Moldova and Serbia. While some of the top contributors by proportion of women continued to be countries deploying only women, their overall personnel numbers remained very small, ranging from 1 to 3 individuals.

### Women in EU CSDP missions and operations

In 2023, EU member states renewed the Civilian CSDP Compact to strengthen the EU’s civilian crisis management missions, committing among other things to increasing women’s representation in line with UN Security Council Resolution 1325. The renewal set the concrete objective of reaching at least 40 per cent women in EU CSDP missions by 2025, with a longer-term goal of parity at all levels—something the previous compact lacked. To support implementation, the EEAS updated the 2021 Strategy and Action Plan, completing the process at the end of 2024. The Strategy and Action Plan to Enhance Women’s Participation in Civilian CSDP Missions 2024–27 is now in place. Nevertheless, progress has been slow and by mid 2025, only 1 of the EU’s 12 civilian missions had met the 40 per cent target: EUBAM Rafah.

### Concluding remarks

#### Since the UN Security Council passed its landmark resolution on women, peace

#### and security 25 years ago, organizations and member states have pledged and

#### set targets to improve the representation of women in peace operations. There

#### has been some progress in this regard, but it remains modest and gender parity

#### is still a distant goal. Overall, women continue to be under-represented in the field.

#### For UN military personnel, both the overall number and the proportion of

#### women have increased. Despite steady gains over the past decade, women’s

#### representation among military experts and staff officers decreased slightly in

#### 2025, although it remained above the year’s intermediate target and on the path

#### towards the 2028 goal of 25 per cent. By contrast, while higher than last year,

#### women’s representation in troop contingents was still low, making the target of

#### 15 per cent by 2028 a challenge at the current pace.

#### In comparison, women’s participation in the UN police is better aligned with

#### the Uniformed Gender Parity Strategy’s 2028 goals. In 2021, the category of

#### individual police officers met the 2028 target of at least 30 per cent women,

#### and the proportion has continued to hover at around 30–32 per cent. In formed

police units, women’s participation surpassed the 2025 target of 15 per cent and has increased steadily since 2021.

#### Although the proportion of women in UN civilian personnel is higher than in

#### the military and police categories, women still remain a minority across all the

#### UN peace operations and field offices analysed, except UNSCOL. UNSCOL was

#### the only case to meet the UN System-wide Strategy on Gender Parity target of

#### 47–53 per cent women in civilian staff. In 2025, the proportion of women in

UN civilian personnel was relatively stable across peace operations and special political missions, with slight increases in both.

#### Five of the 12 OSCE field operations met the target of 45–55 per cent women

#### in their personnel in 2025: the OSCE Centre in Ashgabat, OMIK, OM Moldova,

#### OM Serbia and the OSCE Project Co-ordinator in Uzbekistan. This was 4 more

#### operations than in the previous year. After a few years in which the proportion

#### of women in OSCE field operations was relatively stable, in 2025 it increased by

7.8 percentage points and reached 45 per cent. The number of women in these operations also increased.
#### Over the latest reporting periods, there were decreases in the number of women

#### in civilian EU CSDP missions (to June 2025) and in military EU CSDP missions

#### and operations (to December 2024), partly due to the closures of EUCAP Sahel

#### Niger and EUTM Mali respectively. However, the proportion of women increased

#### in military missions and operations and remained unchanged in civilian

#### missions. Of the 19 missions and operations, only EUBAM Rafah reached at

#### least 40 per cent women in its personnel, consistent with the target set by

#### the renewed Civilian CSDP Compact. Regarding women seconded to EU CSDP

#### missions, 9 contributing countries met or exceeded the 40 per cent target for

#### their seconded personnel: Belgium, Canada, Croatia, Cyprus, Estonia, Finland, Lithuania, Norway and Sweden.

#### The 2025 overview of women’s participation in leadership roles across peace

#### operations shows a mixed picture. Participation increased markedly in the OSCE,

up by 17 percentage points to 25 per cent, and in the UN military, which went

#### from having no woman force commander in 2024 to 1 woman in 2025 (out

#### of 8 force commanders). By contrast, the UN police saw a noticeable decline,

from 40 per cent in 2024 to 20 per cent in 2025. For UN civilian and EU CSDP

#### missions and operations, the proportions were unchanged from last year, at

#### 30 per cent and 15 per cent respectively. The highest proportion of women in

#### leadership roles was observed in UN civilian personnel.

#### This annual overview of women’s representation in peace operations is important

#### for understanding the state of play, particularly related to the strategies and

targets set by organizations deploying peace operations. However, while the numbers are important for fostering discussions on women’s participation, they

#### have limitations and do not address the deeper challenges. A narrow focus on

headcounts can also encourage compliance with targets without addressing underlying constraints in recruitment pipelines, retention, promotion, training

and workplace safety. It can also create situations where women are present in numbers but lack equal opportunities or influence.

#### Alongside tracking representation, organizations need to consider indicators

#### that capture the influence and quality of participation. In line with the WPS

agenda, this focus on participation should advance together with the other WPS pillars of prevention, protection, and relief and recovery.

They should work as mutually reinforcing elements in the integrated implementation of the agenda. Finally, as part of this integrated

effort, it is crucial to simultaneously highlight the need for institutional measures that create enabling conditions for the

#### meaningful and equitable participation of women in peace operations.
