#### 1 Summary

Mass alone is insufficient to overwhelm adversaries. This is the core message of our paper. But wait. Before critics of the ‘quantity is a quality of its own’ narrative prematurely rejoice, both nuance and context must be grasped to fully understand what we mean.

One of the key problems with the ‘mass matters’ movement is that the adversary has a say on the battlefield – and how adversaries respond to attacks with a high number of unmanned systems, for example, very much determines the outcome of the encounter. But response is not a one-way street. How attackers react to a first round of countermeasures very much determines if and to what extent they can accomplish their mission. So, a different approach to concepts and capability development is needed to gauge if, how, and when mass is decisive and how the offense vs defense game is likely to play out.

Developing and implementing this new approach is at the heart of Wild Hornets, an advanced concepts study conducted in cooperation with the German Army Concepts and Capability Development Center (ACCDC). Over the past 18 months we have analyzed how Army aviators flying attack and transport helicopters could best use so-called Air-Launched Effects (ALE) to identify and neutralize adversar- ial Ground-Based Air Defense (GBAD) constellations. To this purpose we have developed a high-fidelity Defense Metaverse, a virtual twin of the battlefield. The Defense Metaverse replicates all parameters of the mission-relevant area of interest to digitally model and simulate the interaction between ALE and GBAD systems as well as the relevant sensors and effectors.

Our Defense Metaverse uses counter-play logic and dynamics to let red and blue forces interact. This, we argue, is the only way for military concept developers to get a sufficient understanding of the tactical added value that different build- ing blocks of future military systems might provide. Furthermore, this approach illustrates that, in interaction with intelligent adversaries, artificial intelligence (AI) can self-learn complex tactical behavior. This pushes the concepts and technology envelope beyond current patterns of refining existing technologies with AI. AI tac- tics open the door to novel behavior on the battlefield to surprise the opponent and gain the upper hand.

Thus, it is not mass, but smart mass that matters – and AI tactics shape smartness. As we demonstrate, self-learned collaborative coordination among the members of a reconnaissance-strike swarm of unmanned systems leverages the principles of war to provide a major leap forward. This approach goes well beyond current swarm concepts. Traditionally, swarm members execute pre-planned flight trajectories and

use relatively simple rules to keep distance from each other, maintain formation, or implement rudimentary cooperation patterns. By contrast, our approach uses distributed control to facilitate cooperation among swarm members to overwhelm adversarial air defense postures. This means that each swarm member follows its own goals and learns how to cooperate with teammates. As a result, cooperation is a function of individual benefit analyses: Swarm members will cooperate, if giving up individual objectives will help them accomplish a common goal of higher value.

These findings propel military performance to new levels as AI tactics that enable self-learned collaborative coordination

e*nhance efficiency* because fewer ALE are needed to engage a GBAD constel- lation, meaning that – potentially – more targets can be attacked with a given number of unmanned assets; *improve effectiveness* by enabling ALE swarms to adjust mission tempo to mission requirements and thus opt for low and slow flight paths to sneak into the GBAD’s ‘dead zone’ for effective engagement; *unlocks emergence* as the ability to instantly respond to unforeseen adversarial moves by defining and delegating tasks and subtasks among members of the swarm at campaign speed.

These outcomes are paramount for missions in the air littoral that constitute the core interest of Wild Hornets. This small air strip is increasingly contested. Control of the air littoral is essential to deliver air power that too often only focuses on blue skies operations while at the same time ensuring ‘protection from above’ for land forces. The AI tactics discussed in this paper illustrate how even very small groups of swarms with only four or five team members can effectively engage even powerful gun-based short-range GBAD solutions like the Gepard system. At the same time, AI tactics also demonstrate how these defender systems can be upgraded to withstand AI-empowered swarms.

Based on these findings we argue that an AI tactics kernel will constitute a key building block for future software-defined defense solutions. This tactics kernel can be used as a multi-avenue transmission mechanism to combine current capa- bility demands with ongoing battlefield requirements and visions of future forces.

This is the second paper in a triology of white papers addressing the role of AI tactics on the battlefield. These papers grow out of GhostPlay, a capability and technology development project aimed at developing red and blue AI tactics. *Free Jazz on the Battlefield,* our first paper published in 2022, laid out the core idea, described key aspects of the basic method – for example regarding the use of decentralized and non-hierarchical command and control – and presented our initial findings focusing on the role of air defense against aggressor swarms. Later this year, we will release our third paper addressing AI tactics for naval combat.

## 2 Boxing Legends on

**the Battlefield**

*Float like a butterfly, sting like a bee;* *his hands can’t hit what his eyes can’t see* Muhammed Ali¹

*Everybody has a plan ‘till they got punched in the mouth* Mike Tyson²

In 2014, then Deputy U.S. Secretary of Defense Robert O. Work and Shawn Brimley were among the first to argue that swarms of unmanned systems are a potential game-changer for future warfighting. Mass, they argued, would provide a new 3 option to overwhelm adversaries and gain the upper hand. Since then, ‘quantity as quality’ has increasingly been interpreted as a leitmotiv for force development in NATO/EU countries and beyond. Most recently, Deputy U.S. Secretary of Defense Kathleen Hicks presented the Replicator initiative that leverages mass for future 4 warfighting. Along this line, the United States have approved the sale of 291 Altius 600M-V loitering munitions and 720 Switchblade 300 to backup Taiwan’s defense 5 posture. Russia’s use of Shahed-136 drones and other unmanned systems in Ukraine⁶ as well as the April 2024 deployment of 300 unmanned assets and missiles by Iran against Israel⁷ seem to vindicate the ‘quantity as quality’ narrative.

Unmanned mass can play an important role to fight a capable peer competitor, for example, by distributing force elements across domains and theaters of opera- tions. But – and this is the key question for force developers – is it as decisive as presumed?

Muhammed Ali and Mike Tyson, the two great boxing champions quoted above, lend a proverbial ‘helping hand’ in answering this question. First, the so-called kill chain⁸ that combines sensors and shooters to identify and hit targets has come to play a foundational role in military thinking. But Ali perfectly captured the sen- sor-to-shooter dilemma when pointing out that the performance of the effectors very much depends on available sensors – and the latency of data and information processing that connects the two. Therefore hands (effectors) can only hit, what eyes (sensors) can see. Mass alone does not provide an easy option out of this di- lemma. Rather, mass increases the challenge to smoothly integrate and coordinate all elements of this sensor-to-shooter cycle.

Second, mass does provide new options to deliver military power, but mass also adds extra burden on force and logistics planners. Mosaic warfare, 9 that foresees a deconstruction of force into single units and a reconfiguration of these units at campaign speed and commensurate with mission requirements, provides the basis for mass to become a decisive factor, but traditional planning is not fit for the ‘Tyson test.’ Planning can only go so far as the knowledge about red and blue forces available at the time of initiating the planning cycle. Spontaneous punches by the enemy, as Tyson rightly observed, cannot be foreseen. Again, only increasing the number of (unmanned) assets without changing the paradigm to coordinate these assets will likely render force engagement against adversaries more cumbersome. Therefore, we argue that there is a pressing need to shift to approaches that are much more cognizant of how to respond to unforeseeable events.

Consequently, caution is at place. Together with the Germany Army Concepts and Capability Development Center (ACCDC) we have been working on Wild Hornets, an 18-month advanced concepts study. We have built a tailored Defense Metaverse, a high-fidelity virtual twin of the battlefield that meets the require- ments of the Center, to develop AI tactics for future concepts on the helicopter use of Air-Launched Effects (ALE), such as Switchblade 600, against Ground- Based Air Defense (GBAD). Based on the findings summarized in this paper, we conclude: On its own, unmanned mass is not decisive. Rather, for mass to prevail, emergent AI tactics, emphasizing formation, not formulation, 10 are needed – in particular when meeting an AI-augmented GBAD equipped with powerful coun- termeasures to fend off attacking ALE swarms.

Our cautionary note on unmanned mass as the new go-to solution for armed forces is important. It highlights that using the Defense Metaverse for concept de- velopment can prevent military planners from being mislead by popular ideas. In addition, our study also illustrates the opportunities that software-defined defense offers to come up with new concepts, specifications for future defense products as well as novel in-service and military training ideas to prepare armed forces for a more assertive, more volatile, and thus also more risk-prone strategic environment.

Counterplay enables AI to develop successful tactics in interaction with a smart adversary. Technically speaking, the system is forward planning a series of play moves to determine the optimal decision sequence. As soon as one actor has developed successful policies, the opponent learns to respond. Counterplay’s goal is to gradually improve the performance of action and decision rules by training them against more complex and more challenging adversaries. In the end, this approach helps define non-exploitable policies.

Counterplay within the Defense Metaverse creates a new opportunity to develop scenarios almost without scripting, as interaction replaces scripting. This provides two key advantages:

First, it is possible to cover a significantly larger number of scenarios than tra- ditional simulations can handle. This includes scenarios illustrating unexpected and surprising behavior, which are most beneficial for concepts development, to assess technical specifications and set the parameters for worst, best, and expected case analyses. While the Defense Metaverse offers AI to operate in the ‘free jazz’ mode without any restrictions, it is also possible to set limits for red forces only to engage within specific and known rules of engagement or to mimic known tactical behavior. Second, counterplay significantly reduces the risk of analytical bias, i.e., design choices for the simulation that are based on specific beliefs/assumptions about adversarial action. Analyst bias is problematic as it may entail conceptual traps, that will normally not be questioned as the respective hypotheses are con- sidered well-established knowledge. But reconfirming what is already known prevents the emergence of non-conventional behavior that would challenge existing wisdom. Red force tactics developed with counterplay, by contrast, produce unexpected outcomes, which may be most important to identify design flaws in workflows of systems used by defenders.

GhostPlay’s approach to counterplay also underpins the development of decen- tralized control strategies, a major element of Mosaic Warfare. 13 As we will discuss later, decentralized control strategies imply that, for example, members of a swarm of unmanned systems learn to develop collaborative tactics to accomplish a mission in tandem. This, in turn, enables the Defense Metaverse to run high-in- tensity combat simulations including a large number of objects that can change their behavior commensurate with the evolution of the simulated scenario – thus pushing the envelope for situations in which blue or red force behavior changes the game in one or the other direction.

It goes without saying that for the Defense Metaverse to offer these simulation options, the digital environment needs to be modeled very realistically. As we discuss the specific parameters relevant for the Wild Hornets concept study in the following section, we want to spend a moment on describing the efforts needed to model the operational environment.

Tactical assertiveness, as outlined in this paper, requires the simulation to process environmental data like terrain, buildings, vegetation, weather, and views. To develop the required level of detail in a relatively short span of time, we have cre- ated automated pipelines. These pipelines use different data to generate a digital model of the target area under consideration (Figure 2).

Building the pipelines starts with using Digital Terrain Elevation Data (DTED), which is georeferenced. The missions analyzed for Wild Hornets were set to play in Franconia, Bavaria (Germany), a region that is quite typical for Central Europe. Information on buildings augmented DTED, and both were fused with Light Detection and Ranging (LIDAR) data. This helped create a terrain and building data profile with a granularity of up to 10cm. We have synthetically generated vegetation to realistically replicate the type and cover of vegetation in the mission area. Depending on the weather conditions to be simulated, trafficability analyses can be conducted.

In preparing data for Wild Hornets, we benefitted from the fact that Bavarian authorities offer open-source digital terrain data. This, however, won’t be the case for many other areas. That’s why we have developed a specific development pipe- line based on photogrammetric procedures and Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) to reconstruct and model three-dimensional infrastructure data based on remote sensing.

#### 4 Wild Hornets: Fine Tuning

#### Development

Wild Hornets is an advanced concepts study conducted in cooperation with the German Army Concepts and Capability Development Center. The purpose of the study is to evaluate future capability requirements for so-called Air-Launched Effects (ALE), unmanned assets occupying the air space between traditional Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS) and missiles. In doing so, the study went beyond off-the-shelf solutions to assess the tactical added value these systems offer in operations against highly performant Ground-Based Air Defense Systems (GBAD). One goal of the analysis was to scrutinize if and to what extent specific technical features such as the ability to exploit terrain features for low and lowest flight paths add tactical value. The second goal was to develop new tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTP) to use ALE. While evaluating existing TTP defined by the Army Center’s experts, we also used AI to develop new TTPs for aggressor swarms and defender GBAD systems.

Before addressing the specific technical parameters set for Wild Hornets, we start by describing the challenges of fighting in the air littoral. This domain is pivotal to ensure the freedom of maneuver of allied forces and deny the enemy the flexibility to change battlefield positions quickly and safely. Lethality, detectability, surviv- ability, and tempo all play a key role in the air littoral – and AI is instrumental in shaping the interplay between these key parameters.

#### 4.1 Fighting in the Air Littoral: Setting the Scene

The air littoral is an increasingly important warfighting domain, but what winning in this narrow air corridor is all about has been under-conceptualized. The air littoral constitutes the ‘air bridge’ between blue skies and the airspace adjacent to land. As such, the air littoral is a bit of a no man’s land as most air forces mainly focus on blue skies whereas land-based air defense capabilities to protect this small strip have atrophied. 14 This was a non-issue during the type of military operations constituting the norm in the past two decades, but it is becoming a major issue with the much more frequent use of UAV, First-Person View (FPV) drones, missiles, and artillery all sharing the same airspace.

As the air littoral is becoming more crowded and contested, the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) launched the Air Space Total Aware- ness for Rapid Tactical Execution (ASTARTE) program in 2020. The goal is to “pro- vide a real-time, common operational picture of the dynamic” future airspace. 15

14 On the contested paradigms informing the division of responsibilities between air and land forces for air littoral operations, see: Bremer/Grieco, “The Air Littoral: Another Look,” pp. 75-78. 15 “Real-time Airspace Awareness and De-confliction of Future Battlespaces.”

A common operational picture that helps deconflicting the use of the air littoral is important but not enough.

Today’s challenges make it paramount to “reconceptualize air control (as) control 16 of the air littoral is rapidly decoupling from that of the blue skies.” This presents an obvious dilemma: losing control of the air littoral cripples air power from below by letting adversaries create a hard-to penetrate air space while at the same time creating heightened dangers from above for the land forces that encroach upon 17 their freedom of maneuver and drastically reduce their survivability.

In addressing dangers in the air littoral, most analyses focus on the advent of UAS swarms. There are heated debates if swarms are going to be successful or not. Justin Bronk and Jack Watling of the Royal United Services Institute (RUSI) correct- ly argue that developing unmanned platforms for a mass precision strike complex requires demanding tradeoffs about airframe, propulsion and power, navigation, 18 datalinks, sensors, effectors and regulatory aspects such as safety requirements. One question, however, is conspicuously absent from the debate: To what extent will future tactics, developed with the use of AI, shape the battlefield behavior of autonomous swarms and how will these AI tactics shape platform, sensor, effector design and characteristics?

This focus on AI tactics reveals a gap in the literature, as most analysts seem 19 unaware that AI can learn to develop battlefield tactics. That’s why the findings of our paper inform military concepts development while at the same time con- tributing to the broader literature. By discussing how AI can self-learn to develop a tactic like collaborative coordination, we show what fighting in the future air littoral could look like. At the same time, we also demonstrate that the offense vs defense debate needs to be much more nuanced as it is anything but straightforward if attacking swarms or ground-based air defenders will gain the upper hand.

##### Vignettes

The vignettes and the core research questions have been defined by the ACCDC. Four vignettes describe the overall situation, the specific mission goals as well as different effector combinations.

To start with an Army aviation unit using four attack and three transport helicopters flies around 20km to the front edge of battle area at below <100ft. Its task is to identify and neutralize an adversarial high-value target. For this purpose, helicop- ters launch several reconnaissance ALEs equipped with optical sensors. The oppo- nent has stationed several Short-Range Air Defense (SHORAD) systems to protect a high value target. Once detected, ALEs attack the SHORAD constellation from different directions to degrade the protection layer that surrounds the high value target. The scenarios modify these core parameters to make the mission increas- ingly complex, competitive, and thus also more risk-prone.

##### Digital SHORAD and Munition Models

The SHORAD system has been modeled using the German Gepard Artillery 20 Anti-Aircraft (AAA) system as the reference. It can be interpreted as a prototyp- ical gun-based SHORAD system like the Russian 2K22 Tunguska and the 96K6 Pantsir or the Chinese Type08 systems. Still today, the Gepard’s overall latency and the powertrains used for the turret and the gun set standards difficult to meet for 21 other gun-based SHORAD systems.

We have also chosen this system because the Gepard’s physical parameters (e.g., traverse time of the turret, firing cadence), the internal processing cycle, and the sensor packages are quite well known. We used the KWS FlakPz2, which did not enter service with the German Army. This system has two periscopes plus optical trackers, thermal image, laser rangefinders, an MDPR 12 search radar comple- 22 mented with a tracking radar and fire control systems.

To simu- late the ballistic curves, we have used a model with quadratic drag. 23

##### Digital ALE Models

ALE systems have been modeled based on AeroVironment’s Switchblade 600 loitering munition (LM). 24 We built two physically sufficient simulation models to implement the flight dynamic. One model represents flights with a flight bearing controller; the second model offers non-stabilized flights directly using the actua- tor control without the flight bearing controller. Although expert pilots considered both models as realistic, we concede that our models are only generic digital rep- resentations of the fixed-wing LM. Therefore, we cannot guarantee how accurately the model represents the flight dynamics of a real-world Switchblade 600. 25

In addition to the Switchblade 600 we have modeled a Non-Line of Sight (NLOS) missile based on the SPIKE NLOS, 26 for which we have built an adequate digital replica. Other types of LM could have been used to construct the ALE swarm, but we consider the Switchblade 600 representative for the research questions under consideration. When technical differences mattered – such as jet propulsion (e.g., Coyote LM²⁷) vs propellors – we have adjusted the specific parameters.

##### Digital Sensor Models

Sensor simulation in our Defense Metaverse is based on signal-based, probabil- istic, and auto-encoder models. Signal-based models provide a realistic replica of the sensor processing model or parts of it. These models also offer plausible high-resolution sensor images, which can be verified. For our simulation we have used signal-based models provided by GhostPlay partner Hensoldt. But sig- nal-based models require significant computing power to simulate physical modes of action. This creates challenges to compute scenarios that need to run faster than real-time. That’s why we have focused on using signal-based models only to analyze specific scenarios.

Sampling and parametrization make it possible to compute probabilistic sensor models that match the performance parameters of signal-based models. Probabil- istic sensor models are very well suited for the use in tactical scenario simulations as they need significantly less computing power. They capture major statistical properties of the signal-based models but are not single-case plausible. Proba- bilistic sensor models have only been used for training runs to develop policies, which were then transferred to operate with signal-based models.

The very high resolution of the GhostPlay Defense Metaverse paves the ground for immersive sensor representation. First, sensor data can be recorded in a real environment to train the auto-encoder. #### 5.1 Setting the Baseline

Before looking at the specific AI tactics developed with Wild Hornets, we want to discuss the simulation-based findings of using state of the art ALE against the SHORAD constellation described above. 30

Initial tests resembled scenarios that most ALE and SHORAD solution providers currently use to demonstrate the performance of their systems. For these tests, helicopters were launching ALE up to 100ft above ground. In some scenarios helicopters provide ALE guidance, in others, ALE swarm members were perform- ing this function. When attacking the targets, ALE were following a descending trajectory. We have used ALE swarms of different size (up to 50 members) to attack SHORAD systems. SHORAD systems were operating from randomly chosen positions and under different day-light conditions to start with. 31 This helped us establishing the baseline to identify the success rate in the basic configuration. 32

Based on 10,000 sample scenarios³³ using different SHORAD constellations, our results suggest the following:

Only in one out of 10 cases will a state-of-the-art ALE using traditional trajecto- ries succeed against the SHORAD constellation shown in the simulation. A SHORAD system using active sensors will be even more powerful as these sensors improve the lead time to detect and engage incoming ALE. Tracks can be automatically initialized if the ALE swarm operates out of ground clutter. Electro-optical (EO) sensors compensate occasional track losses. The performance of a SHORAD system that is manually operated (human in the loop) and uses passive sensors (and laser) decreases significantly when facing a swarm of three to four ALE approaching from different directions. This is mainly due to the zoom-latency to detect, classify, and identify small flying objects, such as small Unmanned Aerial Systems (sUAS). Things change dramatically to the benefit of the defender if the SHORAD system is AI augmented. In this case it is almost impossible for ALE swarms – using conventional trajectories and flying at around 120-180 km/h – to over- whelm the SHORAD constellation. In around 60% of all scenarios, ALE swarms with up to 40 members suffered total destruction.

#### 5.2 AI Tactics for the Endgame

The above results provided the point of departure for further analyses. In line with the requirements of the ACCDC we were interested in identifying the key perfor- mance characteristics future ALE swarms need to meet to overwhelm the simulat- ed SHORAD constellations.

Focusing first on the endgame, when the ALE swarm engages the SHORAD, our simulation results demonstrate that terrain following ALE swarms that approach the target using highly agile flight maneuvers stand a significantly higher chance of success than status quo solutions. Most importantly, however, swarms that self-learn how to coordinate, define, and delegate tasks and sub-tasks among individual swarm members create by far the biggest leap ahead that even highly powerful AI-augmented SHORAD systems have a hard time countering.

##### Terrain Following

For our tests we reconfigured the ALE swarm to become terrain following as shown in Figure 3. This implies that the swarm can adjust the flight altitude to make optimal use of simulated terrain conditions. During the training sessions the control strategy freely set the flight altitude. Neither have we defined specific waypoints nor minimal flight altitudes that the ALE swarm needed to comply with. We only prevented the simulation from overrunning collision with terrain, buildings or vegetation, that caused ALEs to crash.

The existing ground truth provided the basis for the forward-looking coordina- tion function that underpins terrain following. This was an appropriate choice to gauge the required minimal flight altitude that the ACCDC wanted to identify. As the simulator included detailed models of vegetation and infrastructure (e.g., powerlines), standard algorithms could be used to implement dynamic collision detection between objects and environment. We have used Bayesian models to simulate timely recognition of obstacles and turbulences in low altitude flights and obtain realistic loss rates based on flying altitudes. For example, the probability of collision with an object depends on the probability that the object has inadvert- ently not been identified. This, in turn, depends on aspects like distance, tempo

##### Collaborative Self-Coordination

Using different attack vectors to engage the SHORAD constellation with numerous ALEs requires coordination. 37 Across all scenarios, collaborative self-coordination proved to be the most successful feature for ALE swarms to knock out SHORAD systems. This includes the ability to form smaller sub-teams that will perform indi- vidual and collective tasks to accomplish the mission. To do so, the ALE swarm must develop ad hoc strategies that define mission goals and sub goals and delegate the execution of these goals to team members. Algorithmically speaking, this can be done with distributed control, and we have used decentralized partially observable Markov Decision Processes (decPOMDP) to model collaborative coordination. 38

It is very important to understand that collaborative self-coordination, as depicted in Figure 5 with a swarm of three UAS, has nothing to do with traditional swarm- ing and goes way beyond the patterns currently observed in Ukraine. 39 Existing concepts build on the idea that swarm members mimic each other’s behavior to fly specific patterns or execute a pre-planned flight direction. By contrast, distrib- uted control implies that each swarm member follows its own goal. If and to what extent swarm members cooperate depends on the probability that cooperation will make it easier to achieve the mission goal. This requires the swarm to self- learn the best tactic to accomplish the relevant mission – during the end game and in the 20km-runup from the helicopter launch to hitting the target. Collabora- tive self-coordination yields superior results in the simulation:

For a manually operated Gepard, swarms with up to only 6 entities using collaborative time-sensitive coordination are almost impossible to counter. In 8 out of 10 cases a guided ALE swarm able to exploit terrain features needs 7 members to outpace the Gepard. But with collaborative coordination, the size of the ALE swarm shrinks to four members.

##### Agile Flight Maneuvers

Collaborative coordination enables swarms to fly combat jumps or S-like crossings (Figure 6). The main purpose of these flight patterns is to make it more difficult for the SHORAD to track and trace the ALE swarm. In doing so, swarms can attempt to hide in ground clutter, abruptly change the flight path/direction, or move into an attack position that renders the SHORAD weapon system ineffective (beyond elevations of 80°) or exploits weaknesses of the SHORAD’s armor.

For the scenarios we have trained agile flight maneuvers in combination with other patterns. Moreover, we have been cooperating with professional drone racers to record their flight patterns for the end game with the goal to stretch the aerody- namic features of commercial-off-the-shelf FPV drones to the maximum. We used these trajectories to improve the selectivity of the low-level controller, reduce training efforts, and integrate extreme flight patterns into our AI tactics, that are hard to explore because they are more brittle than traditional flight patterns. Initial explorations with these patterns suggest that the ALE swarm’s success rate can be further advanced, in particular when facing a fully automated and AI-augmented SHORAD system.

#### 5.3 AI Tactics for Reconnaissance-Strike Missions

In most cases enemy positions are unknown or hard to detect because of camou- flage, signature management, and other measures undertaken to hide. That’s why reconnaissance missions need to survey the relevant mission area and identify and confirm enemy positions before the ALE swarm enters the end game.

Ideally, the ALE swarm learns to develop a reconnaissance tactic commensurate with the threat landscape and the mission goals. Most importantly, this tactic should be unpredictable and hard (if not impossible) for the adversary to recog- nize and detect. Only if the aggressor ALE swarm remains unnoticed for as long as possible will it be able to approach the SHORAD inside the ‘dead zone’ as discussed above.

Currently, most reconnaissance missions emulate the so-called sweep pattern, which has been developed for search and rescue (SAR) operations. 40 As Figure 7 (left) illustrates, the reconnaissance object flies in a regular pattern over the target – thereby moving like a lawn mower – until the SHORAD location (illustrated by the red star) has been identified. This pattern is effective and efficient – as long as there are no non-cooperative objects on the ground.

**Figure 7: Sweep (left) vs MDP-based (right) Reconnaissance Patterns**

On the battlefield, however, adversarial non-cooperation is the norm. That’s why the sweep pattern is inadequate for combat reconnaissance. It is too easily detect- able and reveals an aggressor’s intentions. Once the SHORAD has detected the pattern, it is easy to anticipate future moves, which improves kill rates. In doing so, defenders do not even have to switch on active sensors, which prevents them from being detected. Therefore, we have developed a new reconnaissance tactic using Markov Decision Processes (MDP) to produce a pattern that appears random and unpredictable (Figure 7, right).

What looks weird at first sight turns out to be powerful when assessing the find- ings of the statistical analysis. If you compare average reconnaissance time with the number of targets to be detected and vary assumptions for false positives and non-detection, it becomes clear that this pattern fully leverages the ‘Muhammed Ali dilemma’ to the benefit of the attacker, because it is much more challenging to understand. In addition, our new MDP reconnaissance pattern also blends prior knowledge about the target area with information about the current situation on the ground. In the end, the MDP-based tactic not only detects SHORAD positions more reliably but also much faster than the sweep approach.

#### 6 Outlook: An AI Tactics

#### Kernel for Software-

#### Defined Defense

The advanced concepts study Wild Hornets, conducted in cooperation with the German ACCDC, illustrates the benefits of using the Defense Metaverse for force development. Our findings show: AI tactics that exploit terrain conditions (terrain following) and advance collaborative coordination can make future ALE swarms much more powerful. In response, AI tactics leveraging insights from game theory can empower air defenders to fend off these swarms.

Right now, the AI tactics developed in the Defense Metaverse are gradually transferred into the physical environment. Together with the Bundeswehr Research Institute for Materials, Fuels, and Lubricants (Wehrwissenschaftliches Institut für Werk- und Betriebsstoffe), we are working on generating adapted tactics that will be used to equip the institute’s future ecosystem of unmanned systems. If success- ful in the real-life setting, these AI tactics will be deployed for operations.

This approach is in line with the German Bundeswehr’s emphasis on software-de- fined defense to augment legacy systems and advance armed forces transfor- 48 mation with the help of synchronized hardware and software development. AI tactics, as argued in this paper, should be interpreted as the core of future software-defined defense solutions. Mature armed forces that already use dif- ferent mission planning/support systems to integrate their assets into the sen- sor-to-shooter cycle can use the AI tactics kernel as an overlay system that fully respects and interacts with legacy software. Armed forces operating at the lower end of the maturity spectrum can use the AI tactics kernel to leapfrog by letting the kernel take over the function of traditional mission planning systems.

As illustrated in Figure 9, the portfolio of software-defined defense solutions is broad. However, most of today’s solutions are input-driven because they empha- size data as a key resources armed forces need to deal with. In contrast, the con- cept of an AI tactics kernel is impact driven: Rather than asking what is available to accomplish a military mission, the AI tactics kernel asks what impact the mission is expected to deliver. The two perspectives are complementary but emphasize different aspects.

This distinction also paves the ground for a new tactics stack to complement the technology stack of software-defined defense. The tactics stack offers novel ways to finetune and deliver military power by interpreting tactics as the value layer that armed forces, which use a service-oriented architecture, can move across domains and operating levels. The tactics stack advances battlefield versatility by coordinating the behavior of troop elements, platforms, and payloads like sensors and effectors.
