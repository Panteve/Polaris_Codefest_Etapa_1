**THIRTY-EIGHTH PERIODIC REPORT OF THE SECRETARY GENERAL TO THE** **PERMANENT COUNCIL ON THE ORGANIZATION OF AMERICAN STATES** **MISSION TO SUPPORT THE PEACE PROCESS IN COLOMBIA (MAPP/OAS)**

The following report is submitted pursuant to Resolution CP/RES. 859 (1397/04), by which the Permanent Council of the Organization of American States (OAS) requests that the Secretary General report on the work of the OAS Mission to Support the Peace Process in Colombia¹ (MAPP/OAS or the Mission) on a regular basis.

The information² covers the findings obtained between July 1 and December 31, 2024, a period during which the Mission carried out 943 field missions, covering 275 population centers in 171 municipalities in 25 of the country’s departments. These population centers included municipal capitals, administrative districts (*corregimientos*), villages (*veredas*), indigenous reserves (*resguardos*), and community councils. During this period, MAPP/OAS teams traveled 130,100 kilometers by land and 1,527 kilometers by river.

The OAS General Secretariat (GS/OAS) wishes to highlight and express its gratitude for the financial and political support of the member states and observers that make up the “Basket Fund”—Canada, Chile, Germany, Ireland, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States—which makes the presence of the MAPP/OAS possible. The international community’s steady support is a determining factor in the short and medium term for Colombia and is essential to ensure the Mission’s sustainability in the territories. The GS/OAS likewise recognizes the contributions of Norway and Switzerland, which strengthen the work of the MAPP/OAS in essential areas such as citizen participation and dialogue. Finally, it thanks the governments of Argentina, Germany, and Switzerland for providing talent through 3 secondments of personnel, people who contribute to the country and the Mission with their values and expertise.

The GS/OAS also warns about the expansion and diversification of strategies of forced recruitment, use and abuse of children and adolescents, and sexual violence against them by armed groups. Public servants were also victims of homicides, forced displacements, threats, and extortion by armed groups, which led to a weakened institutional presence in the territories. It also reiterates that it is imperative to ensure the protection of civil servants and ensure favorable working conditions. In spite of the state's efforts to prevent harm and protect social leaders, there was still a high risk involved in engaging in community action and defending the environment, human rights, and the territory, among other advocacy efforts, mainly in areas of territorial disputes between armed groups and in places where military and humanitarian actions were carried out.

In border areas, the Colombian population and migrants were victims of homicides, kidnappings, human trafficking, migrant smuggling, sexual violence, forced disappearance, and enlistment by armed groups. As a result of different measures implemented by the states, the Colombian population perceived that along the border with Panama there was a decrease in the overland flow of migrants seeking to reach the United States. This is in contrast with the trend seen in Buenaventura in Valle del Cauca, where authorities and communities pointed to an increase in irregular transit toward the same destination.

Against this backdrop, the GS/OAS urgently calls for an end to the violence in the country and for the implementation of coordinated, contextualized, comprehensive, and preventive actions geared toward protecting the civilian population in urban settings, border areas, and especially in rural areas. The interdependence and transnational impact of the problems of violence are exacerbating security challenges in the region. Confronting these threats and strengthening peace in Colombia is a shared responsibility that requires a renewed commitment from the international community, especially from the countries of the region.

The actions of armed groups continued to cause environmental damage to strategic ecosystems. Deforestation and contamination of water sources due to the illegal extraction of mineral deposits and the discharge of hydrocarbons and chemicals used in the processing of cocaine base (*pasta base de coca*) were some of the effects caused in Antioquia, Arauca, Bolívar, Caquetá, Cauca, Chocó, Córdoba, Guaviare, Meta, Nariño, Norte de Santander, Putumayo, and Valle del Cauca. In some regions within these departments, armed groups continued to impose restrictions on the use and care of natural resources.

The preservation of biodiversity and the protection of strategic ecosystems require urgent and sustainable actions, as well as the implementation of the commitments made by Colombia under the Escazú Agreement on preventing risks and protecting environmental leaders.

On another point, Colombian institutions have made it a priority to address social conflicts as a pillar for peacebuilding in Colombia. This commitment requires making progress toward appropriate linkages, coordination, and complementarity among the national, departmental, and

municipal levels; it also requires the definition of legal tools and the strengthening of capacities for social dialogue, which will allow the development of sustainable agreements.

As they seek to overcome challenges and consolidate achievements in the search for peace, Colombians are not alone. For all Colombians and for their rights, the GS/OAS renews its commitment to continue supporting initiatives to implement and territorialize the Total Peace Policy. The will and solidarity manifested in the presence of the MAPP/OAS is a reminder of the value of every action on the road to a peace that transcends time and results in more rights for more people in the Americas.

**3. HARMFUL IMPACTS ON THE CIVILIAN POPULATION¹⁷** The armed groups continued to violate the rights to life, liberty, personal integrity, security, and property of the civilian population. In situations involving armed confrontation, the groups failed to respect the principle of distinction and used educational institutions, land parcels, and homes as places of refuge from military actions. They also took food for themselves, took control of property used to provide public services,
18 and forced civilians to provide them with lodging, sell them products, and store weapons.

In Antioquia, Caquetá, Norte de Santander, and Valle del Cauca, the armed groups left messages and graffiti alluding to their presence on homes, gas stations, and vehicles. Their use of civilian property put the communities at risk of being stigmatized as informants or collaborators of other groups. In Arauca, Antioquia, Huila, Nariño, and Norte de Santander, the groups attacked commercial establishments, vehicles, and homes with bursts of gunfire and explosive devices in retaliation against the civilian population for rebuffing threats and impositions of payment and with the intention of harassing members of enemy groups.

The armed groups took houses, farms, and commercial establishments from the civilian population. These properties were used for cattle ranching, setting up camps, planting illicit crops, and extracting mineral deposits. This impact took the form of direct purchase of land by members of the groups, 19 the surrender of land as payment for impositions, 20 and the abandonment of land due to forced displacement by intimidation. In Catatumbo, women heads of household were threatened with the forced recruitment of their sons and daughters if they did not abandon their property.

In parts of Antioquia, Arauca, Córdoba, Chocó, Guaviare, Meta, Nariño, Norte de Santander, Putumayo, Tolima, and Valle del Cauca, forced displacements occurred on both an individual and mass scale; 21 these particularly affected minors, women, teachers, peace signatories, and campesino and ethnic communities. These population groups were forced to abandon their homes, livelihoods, and community environments in search of protection from the risk of being victims of other harmful impacts. Despite the complex security conditions, some families returned to their territories without institutional support, given the precariousness and perceived risk in the places where they had taken refuge, or returned temporarily to check the condition of their property.

PEACE INITIATIVES, DISCUSSIONS, AND DIALOGUES** The Total Peace Policy made progress in implementing its territorial approach in several initiatives, discussions, and dialogues between the national government and the armed groups. These processes have aimed to establish agreements to put an end to the harmful impacts on the civilian population, ensure the presence of institutions for the transformation of the territories, and guarantee the rights of the victims, as well as to strengthen confidence in institutions and the legitimacy of the peace policies. The national government faces the challenge of consolidating a strategy of political, communicational, and operational coordination for the implementation of the processes and compliance with the agreements reached. This requires budget allocations specifically earmarked for peace, for assertive institutional communication, and the participation of mayors' and governors' offices in the initiatives, discussions, and dialogues. It continues to be essential for the parties to take on and fulfill commitments to ensure the security of communities and public servants in the territories prioritized for implementation of the agreements. Similarly, there is still a need to adjust and create legal frameworks that facilitate the transition to civilian life for members of the armed groups. The peace agreements should also consider strengthening the ordinary criminal justice system to ensure, in addition to transitional justice, the proper prosecution of the most serious and representative crimes and of those who do not meet the requirements for access to transitional mechanisms. This would contribute to the legal sustainability of the agreements and would help overcome impunity.

**5.1. Comprehensive Rural Reform (RRI)** Implementation of Point 1 on Comprehensive Rural Reform in the Final Peace Agreement
(*Acuerdo Final de Paz*, AFP) was strengthened with the territorialization strategy of the National Agrarian Reform and Rural Development System through the formation of Departmental and Municipal Agrarian Reform Committees in Arauca, Bolívar, Cauca, Córdoba, and Putumayo. 62

The national government has the challenge of coordinating new regulatory spheres such as Protection Areas for Food Production, Campesino Agri-food Territories (*Territorios Campesinos* *Agroalimentarios*, TECAM), and Temporary Natural Resource Reserve Zones (*Zonas de Reserva* *de Recursos Naturales de Carácter Temporal*). **5.5. Transitional Justice⁸⁴**
85 Nearly 20 years after the enactment of Law 975 of 2005, the special criminal proceeding under the Justice and Peace Law has revealed important aspects of the truth about the armed conflict in Colombia, including the scale of the harm inflicted on the civilian population and the judicial responsibility of economic and political actors. The Justice and Peace Chambers (*Salas* *de Justicia y Paz*) have prosecuted 75,530 victimizing acts and have recognized 56,957 victims 86 by means of 80 final judgments.

Building on this progress, the Special Jurisdiction for Peace and the Justice and Peace system have worked in coordination, focusing on clarifying the facts and protecting the rights of victims. For example, they have held technical roundtables for the exchange of information on macro- criminal patterns and routes for identification of perpetrators. Specifically, the JEP has used the judgments handed down in the context of the Justice and Peace system as input for studying 87 requests for justice.

Despite the progress made, challenges remain to achieve the satisfaction of victims' rights, overcome impunity for crimes committed in the context of the armed conflict, and guarantee due 88 process. One of these challenges is the time it has taken to resolve the cases, due to the adoption of procedural methods typical of the ordinary justice system for the prosecution of those who are most responsible, hampering the comprehensive reparation of the victims.

It is also key to ensure the financing of the UARIV Reparation Fund for Victims in order to comply with the payment of compensation, recognized and still to be recognized through judgments. Another challenge is to consolidate effective coordination between the different jurisdictions and entities in charge of transitional justice and comprehensive reparations for 89 victims.

Overcoming these challenges will entail identifying and adjusting procedural and budgetary aspects, seeking a balance between processing time, victims' rights, the legal certainty of applicants and those who appear before the courts, and institutional capacity.

Political and financial support for peace in Colombia represents a unique opportunity to halt and reverse the loss of biodiversity in strategic ecosystems and take action against climate change.

The national government, in fulfillment of its obligations as a state party to the Convention, presented its Biodiversity Action Plan 2030 90 in which it recognizes the relationship between armed conflict, illegal networks, and biodiversity loss. The Action Plan includes the promotion of the agroforestry economy and the implementation of the Final Peace Agreement, as well as steps to prevent nature from continuing to represent a pressure factor for armed groups in the ongoing peace talks.

COP16 recognized the valuable role played by indigenous, Afro-descendent, and campesino communities in the conservation of biodiversity; at the same time, it warned that they are victims of extraordinary risk for carrying out activities in defense of their territories and due to the precarious implementation of the prevention and protection measures to which the Colombian state committed itself under the Escazú Agreement.

The incorporation of environmental issues in the peace talks identified the need for regulatory changes to address the tension between environmental protection and the economic development of communities living in Forest Reserve Zones and National Natural Parks. An update of the types of protection in Forest Reserve Zones, the regularization of the carbon bond market, and the draft legislation on traceability of deforestation-free livestock production are some of the initiatives that would facilitate the incorporation of an environmental sustainability approach in the rural development of the territories most affected by the armed conflict.

In Bajo Cauca, the Ministry of Mines and Energy created the first Agro-mining District to formalize mining activities, protect ecosystems, and generate an economy based on food production that discourages communities' dependence on extractive activities. The limited enforcement of environmental and mining regulations is conducive to the proliferation of illicit economies, so this concept represents an opportunity that can be replicated in other parts of the country where the extraction of mineral deposits is also associated with the presence of armed groups.

The implementation of judicial operations against deforestation and illegal roads in the northern part of the Serranía de Chiribiquete National Park highlighted the need for regulatory changes to advance agreements between environmental institutions and communities to reduce deforestation in the national park. After the operations, the armed groups increased restrictions to entry for environmental officials and programs to protected areas in Caquetá, Guaviare, Meta, and Putumayo.

Illegal mining continued to contaminate water sources and lead to deforestation in Caquetá, Cauca, and Valle del Cauca, as well as in the regions of Bajo Cauca and northeastern Antioquia, southern Bolívar, San Juan and Atrato, southern Córdoba, Telembí, and Bajo Putumayo.

In these territories, the authorities and communities noted an increase in the entry and use of backhoes, which contaminated and diverted riverbeds, causing soil erosion and loss of biodiversity. This has encouraged some families to depend on extractive activities over agriculture and fishing to ensure their food security.

**7. PARTICIPATION AND CAPACITY BUILDING FOR SOCIAL DIALOGUE** Colombian institutions have placed a priority on addressing social conflicts in order to make progress in peacebuilding. Making this commitment a reality will require coordination and complementarity between the national, departmental, and municipal levels; it will also require defining legal tools and strengthening capacities for dialogue and the construction of sustainable consensus among diverse sectors for the transformation of social conflicts, under the premise of *talk so as to agree, agree so as to comply, comply so as to transform.* The Attorney General’s Office issued Directive 0001 of 2024
92 on the recognition, guarantee, and protection of the fundamental right to peaceful social protest enshrined in the Constitution. Among other things, the directive established that no one may be investigated or prosecuted for calling for or exercising the right to protest within the limits of the legal framework, and stated that, as a general rule, public security forces should not use violent means to break up protests.

Legal actions against the extraction of mineral deposits and the increase in the price of diesel fuel were the basis for some of the social protests that limited the free movement of goods and people. The authorities and protesters resolved these mobilizations in a peaceful and negotiated manner.

The reactivation of some of the social dialogue opportunities responded to the need to address commitments taken on by previous administrations after protests and strikes in the territories. For example, the Ministry of the Interior revitalized the dialogue roundtable to follow up on the Panoya agreements in the cordillera of Nariño, after three years without concrete results and following the communities' announcement about the resumption of protests. However, the availability of comprehensive institutional services was limited by the absence of some ministries and government agencies that had been convened, as well as by the scant prior coordination and the lack of information on follow-up to the agreed actions.

In this context, the MAPP/OAS implemented processes to strengthen capacity in social dialogue, conflict transformation, strategy design, and the use of analytical tools. These training exercises were geared toward the social dialogue teams at the Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development, the Rural Development Agency, and the National Land Agency. The Mission recognizes their political will and commitment to strengthen their capacities for the implementation of the Comprehensive Rural Reform.

The implementation of PAN 1325 is an opportunity to strengthen the capacities of women delegates in the peace talks, as well as women representing the security forces in peace processes. The weak coordination between PAN 1325 and the Total Peace Policy hinders the incorporation of the gender and peace agenda into the protocols, agreements, and territorial transformation plans derived from the peace talks and the discussion forums on social and legal issues.

The Inter-American Commission of Women of the Organization of American States, the MAPP/OAS, and the Vice Ministry of Women held the "Cristina Bautista Taquinas" Course for Female Candidates to Regular Elections and Indigenous Peoples’ Governments. This experience, in which the Mission actively participated, was an achievement in terms of intersectionality and capacity building for the participation and political advocacy of indigenous women from different countries of the Americas. 95

In July, the Action Plan 2024-2026 of the Comprehensive Program of Guarantees for Women Leaders and Human Rights Defenders was presented. The Human Rights Directorate of the Ministry of the Interior made progress in its territorialization process, with the participation of more than 800 women in 16 departments. 96 In some of these territories, the Ministry and local authorities formulated Territorial Action Plans to guide coordination among institutions and the implementation of concrete actions by mayors' and governors' offices.

These steps forward are evidence of the national government's efforts to promote actions with gender-differentiated measures within the framework of human security. However, the challenge of prevention and protection of women leaders and their families continues, given the escalation in the number of harmful impacts to them due to the armed conflict. The protection of women leaders includes providing guarantees regarding their labor rights, access to land, education, and economic autonomy, among other areas.
