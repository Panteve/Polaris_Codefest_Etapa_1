This research policy paper explores opportunities for export control authorities to adopt AI and DLT—and to adapt to the adoption of these technologies by exporters—in order to reduce biological weapon proliferation risks. #### I. The role of national export control authorities in preventing the proliferation of biological weapons

Under Article III of the 1972 Biological Weapons Convention (BWC), the states parties undertake to neither ‘transfer to any recipient’ proscribed biological agents, toxins, weapons, equipment or means of delivery nor ‘in any way to assist, encourage, or induce any State’ to ‘manufacture or other wise acquire’ them. 4 Under Article X, the states parties also ‘undertake to facilitate, and have the right to participate in, the fullest possible exchange of equipment, materials and scientific and technological information for the use of bacteriological (biological) agents and toxins for peaceful purposes’. To meet their BWC commitments, many states have set up export control systems that enable oversight of international transfers of relevant patho gens, toxins and dual-use biological equipment and related technology and software. Their aim is to prevent transfers destined for proscribed biological weapon-related activities while safeguarding legitimate trade. As the implemen tation of export controls creates administrative burdens for exporters, it is important that states operate export control systems efficiently in order to limit—to the extent possible—any disruptions to legitimate trade. The main multilateral forum through which states coordinate their export controls on items related to biological (and chemical) weapons is the Aus tralia Group. It currently has 42 participating states and the European Union (EU). 5 The participating states agree on common guidelines for the responsible transfer of relevant dual-use items, maintain and update common control lists that define such items, and share their experiences with the implementation of the controls. 6 Each Australia Group participant

The national authorities that contribute to a state’s export control system can generally be divided up into (*a*) licensing authorities, (*b*) investigative and enforcement agencies, and (*c*) intelligence agencies. Licensing authorities process (i.e. receive, assess, and grant or deny)—export licence applications. Investigative and enforcement agencies include national customs services, police services and prosecutor’s offices. National intelligence agencies, in particular foreign intelligence services, compile general country situation reports and risk assessments that can feed into both licence assessments and customs risk assessments. They also collect specific information on diversion and proliferation activities. National export licensing authorities receive large amounts of data as part of licence applications and as part of the exchanging of intelligence, risk assessment and customs data with other domestic export control authorities. AI technologies can help process and analyse large amounts of such struc tured and unstructured data, and DLT can improve auditability of digital transactions through distributed record-keeping. 7

#### II. Emerging technology use cases for export control authorities

Neither AI nor distributed ledger-based technology has yet been widely adopted by national export control authorities to strengthen the implemen tation of their national export control systems. However, studies and pilot projects have begun identifying and testing a range of possible use cases. These use cases are supported by a growing number of examples of deploy ments of AI and DLT in related areas that can serve as case studies for aspects of their usability, utility and limitations. The following three use cases outline specific challenges faced by export control authorities, related practices and, where applicable, current technologies in use. Each use case discusses how AI or DLT could be used to address specific challenges, and their current limitations. Notably, the three use cases discussed here do not represent an exhaustive account of relevant applications of AI and DLT but are rather a selection for illustrative purposes.

**Use case 1. Using DLT to verify strategic trade flows and the authenticity of associated documentation**

The implementation of export controls for biological (and other) dual-use items involves exporters compiling a set of documents to submit as part of the licence application. These provide key information about the item and about the declared end use and end user, and possible assurances from the recipient or national authorities of the recipient state, among others. *The risk of falsification of documentation and obscuring of transaction parties*

Previous illicit procurements of materials related to nuclear, biological or chemical weapons have, for example, sought to circumvent export controls by creating the appearance of a plausible arrangement of intermediaries that leads to a legitimate end user or which does not involve items that are subject to export controls. 9 Proliferators make use of front companies that present a legitimate image and take advantage of critical nodes such as free ports and transit and trans-shipment hubs that can make tracing and verification of shipments more difficult. They also frequently falsify or tamper with required documents such as end-use or end-user certificates (e.g. by removing a clause prohibiting re-export). In these ways they compromise the integrity of the transaction and the broader supply chain that the export licensing process and the attached documentation seek to assure. 10 The relevant national authorities may struggle to detect falsified docu ments submitted during licensing, trans-shipment, customs clearance or possible re-transfer. Streamlining and securing the exchange of information from the different contributing authorities and the processing of and secure access to all documentation accumulated related to a specific transfer is still difficult for many states. 11

##### DLT for authenticated licensing documentation

While a growing number of states have adopted electronic export licensing and customs declaration systems, the use of paper documentation is still widely accepted. In states with less advanced export control systems, it can be the standard. Even in states that have implemented electronic systems and software, there is a broad variety in capabilities, interoperability and inte gration between the various national authorities. In this situation, adopting a DLT system for all of a state’s licensing documen tation is currently neither realistic nor desirable. However, adopt ing DLT systems (e.g. blockchain) for specific licensing documentation has been proposed to address persisting challenges. It can, for example, help the secure management of sensitive data, the detection of falsified documents and identities, and the tracing of individual items.12 Some commercial sec tors have already adopted DLT to ensure the provenance and authenticity of items, including fresh produce, pharmaceuticals, artworks and diamonds. 13

*A permissioned blockchain for end-use and end-user documentation*

One particularly promising use case would be the creation of a permissioned blockchain—that is, a closed network with additional control layers that limit access to information on the ledger to authenticated users with varying levels of permission. The permissioned blockchain would be operated by a national licensing authority and exporters would be required to exchange end-use and end-user certificates using this platform. 15 Using a blockchain for this particular aspect of export controls could help improve verification of the exporter’s identity, the authenticity of end-use certificates and the end user’s identity, while maintaining privacy and chain-of-custody standards. While a DLT cannot prevent the submission of a false digital identity by a transaction party or a falsified document, it can significantly increase bar riers by requiring digital verification of identity when adding parties to the blockchain system, as well as timestamping and hashing each individual transaction and each modification of the documentation. 16 This ensures the immutability of official documents, the traceability of any falsification attempts and permanent evidence of users attempting to use a false digital identity. Where digital assets are created to correlate with physical assets and to allow them to be traced and audited, including with a view to possible prosecutions of violations, such DLT solutions could simplify detection and prosecution and help deter violations.

##### Use case 2. Using AI to support classification of items

A key step in export control implementation is determining whether an item is subject to control. For physical goods, this usually involves comparing the technical specifications of an item with the parameters provided in relevant control list items. Controls on software are usually based on specific tech nical capabilities or the software being ‘specially designed or modified for the “development”, “production” or “use”’ of another controlled item. 17 In the case of technology—that is, information, in the form of technical data or technical assistance, required for the development, production or use of a product—determining whether controls apply is often particularly difficult. National authorities have to be able to verify that the exporter has correctly classified the item (i.e. a good, software or technology) or if a false classifi cation was submitted.

*Keeping up with the demands of export licensing*

Exporters expect the licensing process to be fast and efficient in order to pre vent delays and uncertainties and thus competitive disadvantage. However, ensuring reasonable processing times while maintaining effective oversight is constantly demanding for many national authorities as volumes of trade grow, control lists and rationales for controls expand, and ever more intangible transfers of technology and software become subject to control. 18 Human, financial and technical resources of national export control authorities have not grown in line with the demands in most states, resulting in longer pro cessing time and reduced availability, for example, of classification assistance and direct engagement with exporters. Many electronic licencing systems lack the technical capabilities to inter face with information and data provided by other domestic export control authorities, which limits information exchange among the different author ities. 19 Upgrading or even replacing an electronic licensing system is costly, time-intensive and a major project for any licensing authority. Many states provide a range of means to facilitate licensing by, for example, issuing gen eral export authorizations for less sensitive transactions—these then allow exporters to ship multiple items to multiple destinations without having to reapply for a licence every time. However, even when guidance material on the use of these general licences is available, many exporters, particularly small and medium-size enterprises, struggle to determine whether they are eligible to use the general licence and to comply with its reporting require ments. As a result, they often rely on applying for single licences, increasing the case load for licensing authorities. 20

*AI for classification of items and easing bottlenecks*

Some states have begun exploring the use of AI in internal research or pilot projects or for narrow applications in the export control process. Customs authorities appear to be adopting AI-enabled solutions at a higher rate both for classification of items by tariff codes and for probabilistic risk assessment in customs screening and inspections. 21 In contrast, only a small number of export licensing authorities have announced their adoption of AI tech nologies or have shared experiences from internal research and pilot projects. The most concrete example, to date, of a national licensing authority using an AI solution to assist classification of dual-use items comes from South Korea, where the Korean Institute of Nuclear Nonproliferation and Control (KINAC) has deployed the Intelligent Export Control Review System (IXCRS) to perform an initial classification of large volumes of items associated with the supply of nuclear power plants or research reactors. 22

A promising use case for AI technology in export control authorities is the use of a large language model (LLM) to perform an internal initial classifi cation of an item based on the information provided by the exporter in a licence application. An LLM that has been custom trained on a sufficient amount of data on previous applications and classifications could be used to provide a first verification indication of the classification done by the exporter and a contextual analysis of whether submitted documents share character istics with previous false classifications or falsified documents. Such an LLM would not be public facing and would be trained only on selected data. A human licensing official would still need to review and take the decision on the classification and what it means for the licensing process and decision. Building on AI-assisted classification, another use case would be an AI system that helps flag where a general licence could be used instead of several single licences and thus help reduce the burden on licensing authorities. Notably, cybersecurity requirements place considerable limitations to the use of such AI tools, as do legal limitations on the extent to which a licensing authority might be allowed to use information provided in previous licence appli cations as training data. Any lack of an application programming interface (API) between the software and systems used by a state’s different export control authorities may also limit the utility and effectiveness of such tools. 23

##### Use case 3. Adapting to the use of AI-enabled compliance tools by exporters

Exporters of biological dual-use items—which could be major life science or pharmaceutical companies, start-ups in the biotechnology ecosystem, or laboratories, research institutes or universities—face challenges in com plying with export controls. For large companies or research institutes, identifying the specific transactions subject to licence requirements among the significant volumes of transactions is difficult. The classification of items (as discussed in Use case 2) can also be complicated for exporters. Especially in the biotechnology sector, exporters already need to classify emerging technologies during the research and development phases and understand whether an export might occur. This requires considerable expertise and capacities as it often involves transfers of technical data, technical assistance or know-how. 24 Transaction party screening (including for restrictive meas ures)—which involves screening the end user and end use of the item con sidered for export—can also take significant time and require specific language skills that an exporter may lack.

The integration of LLM-enabled tools into compliance workflows should not replace human judgement. Instead, it could help non-compliance staff provide information required for item classification, transaction party screening and other internal compliance procedures. *Adaptation and guidance provision by national authorities*

Although the use of AI tools to support compliance is largely still in a develop mental stage, some products are already commercially available. These include both tools for item classification and tools for transaction party screening. 30 At the same time, AI solutions could be misused to spoof or specifically counter the approach and methods of systems used by national export control authorities. For example, dual-use items could be misclassified in order to avoid detection. This could void some of the opportunities offered by AI. #### III. The way forward

Adoption of AI and DLT by national export control authorities depends on many factors, including resources, innovation culture, bureaucratic politics and incentivization. They have been cautious in their exploration of AI tools for the implementation and enforcement of export controls, with cyber- and data-security considerations being of particular concern. The deployment of DLT continues to be discussed, in particular for applications in the wider supply-chain security framework. However, the momentum for its deploy ment in export control applications has markedly slowed down compared to the drive for the adoption of AI technologies. Meanwhile, among compliance professionals, the development and marketing of AI-enabled trade compliance solutions—in particular product- classification tools—is spreading much more quickly. However, there are still significant limitations to the available tools, particularly for application in research- and innovation-heavy fields such as biotechnology. They cannot— and will probably never—substitute for adequate resourcing and final human assessment. To help reap the benefits of AI and DLT in export control applications, states should facilitate the sharing of experiences between their national export control authorities. The Australia Group is one forum through which the participating states could share and discuss experiences, concerns and lessons learned from pilot projects that explore the adoption of AI-enabled tools and DLT systems by their national export control authorities. This could feed into and inform discussions within the wider framework of the BWC, thereby contributing to possible standard-setting and the strengthening of compliance with strategic trade controls around the world. Enabling global access to appropriate tools that make use of AI or DLT is essential. It could help increase both effectiveness and efficiency and thus reduce the impact of export controls on legitimate international trade in biological materials and related equipment, software and technology for peaceful purposes. The adoption of emerging technologies by compliance professionals equally challenges national export control authorities to be prepared for assessing and auditing internal compliance programmes that use such tools.
