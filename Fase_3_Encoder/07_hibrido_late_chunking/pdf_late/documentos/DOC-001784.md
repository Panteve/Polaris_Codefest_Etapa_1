#### I. High-containment laboratories

National biosafety and biosecurity frameworks combine legal, regulatory and institutional measures that ensure safe and secure management of biological materials. They include standards for laboratory practices, containment and oversight. Approaches to these frameworks necessarily reflect local public health priorities, resource constraints, and linguistic or institutional differ ences. There is no single global model; what matters is functional equivalence and sustained engagement. Governance of laboratories is a core element of biosecurity as these facil ities increasingly serve as access points to dual-use capabilities. High-containment laboratories are classified as biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) or biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) based on the level of containment required to safely handle infectious agents. BSL-3 facilities handle pathogens that can cause serious disease (e.g. West Nile virus or the bacteria responsible for tuberculosis) using enhanced respiratory protection and restricted access. BSL-4 laboratories work with especially dangerous agents capable of causing life-threatening disease (e.g. the Ebola or Marburg viruses) under maximum containment conditions. The global expansion of HCLs has outpaced the establishment of consist ent oversight, which has created uncertainty about how safety and security standards are applied. Three persistent gaps continue to undermine inter national confidence in safe and peaceful HCL operation.

1. *Transparency mechanisms are weak*, which limits understanding of biosafety and biosecurity practices across borders.
2. *There is no common regulatory compliance model*, which makes it difficult to establish international standards for safety and security.
3. *Oversight capacity is uneven*, with many states lacking sustained resources for inspection, training and enforcement.
##### Transparency

Visibility into HCL operations has not kept pace with their rapid expansion, which has reduced confidence in peaceful intent. Uneven reporting also underscores the broader challenge of sustaining politically binding but unenforced trans parency measures within the BWC.

##### Regulation and governance

How HCLs are governed has direct implications for international trust in compliance with the BWC. Unlike other arms control treaties, the BWC lacks a verification mechanism to confirm that its states parties are complying with treaty commitments. Oversight therefore depends largely on national systems. However, these systems often differ widely in scope and rigour. Coordination and accountability among states remain limited, particularly in how HCL operations are regulated and monitored. According to the global mapping study, 131 of the 143 countries (91 per cent) with BSL-3 facilities reviewed in 2024 had no national guidance on oversight of dual-use research or on responsible science practices. 7 While many BWC states parties main tain internal mechanisms or legal measures that are not visible through open-source review, this figure nevertheless suggests that national oversight of these HCLs remains uneven. Regulatory standards for high-containment facilities also vary signifi cantly. Some states operate formal licensing and inspection regimes, while others rely on informal or outdated regulatory arrangements. 8 Compliance responsibilities are often split across multiple ministries or agencies—health, science, agriculture, security or defence—without a single coordinating authority. While regulatory diversity is inevitable given differences in lan guage, institutional culture and health priorities, it still complicates mutual understanding and baseline transparency.

Persistant gaps between national oversight of biosafety and biosecurity practices and international mechanisms intended to promote transparency and accountability hinder progress. Working papers submitted to the BWC Working Group on the Strengthening of the Convention and a resolution adopted in 2024 by the World Health Assembly acknowledge these limi tations, but practical mechanisms for translating global commitments into coordinated national action remain limited. 9 These coordination challenges are most visible in states operating high- containment or advanced research facilities, but the underlying principles of biosafety, biosecurity and responsible oversight apply to all research environ ments. Few low- or middle-income countries currently host BSL-3 or BSL-4 laboratories, but numbers may rise as global capacity expands. Many already operate smaller diagnostic and research facilities that handle potentially hazard ous biological materials. Strengthening traceability, documentation and personnel accountability in such facilities can reinforce national implemen tation of the BWC and support broader public health resilience. Framing biosafety and biosecurity improvements as part of shared capacity-building and health-system initiatives can also make international engagement more relevant and attainable acrose diverse national contexts.

##### Oversight complications of new technologies

Rapidly advancing technologies are further complicating HCL management as they reshape laboratory practice itself. Automation and robotics reduce the need for direct human contact with pathogens, while AI systems are increasingly used to support routine monitoring, tracking of equipment performance and analysis of biological data. Synthetic biology and DNA- design tools allow laboratories to generate novel genetic material on-site, while advanced storage systems expand the range of biological agents that can be stored and reproduced. 10 These tools reduce direct human involvement, thereby potentially reducing the ability to trace how biological materials are being used. Traditional assurance mechanisms such as facility registration or peri odic self-reporting remain essential, yet they were designed for more static research environments and struggle to capture dynamic, real-time laboratory activity in today’s increasingly distributed research systems. #### II. Emerging technology use cases for high-containment laboratories

##### Use case 1. Using AI and DLT to address risks associated with gene synthesis

Advances in gene synthesis—the design and production of synthetic DNA sequences—are lowering technical and financial barriers to creating and modifying genetic material. 13 Portable sequencing tools, benchtop gene printers and cloud-based ‘biofoundry’ services enable researchers to create, modify and assemble genetic material in routine laboratory environments. This has expanded access to techniques once limited to advanced research institutions. While this diffusion supports innovation, it also raises the risk that genetic material related to dangerous pathogens could be manipulated without appropriate oversight. However, access to and expertise in gene syn thesis remain uneven, particularly in low-resource settings where technical and financial barriers persist.

##### Oversight of gene synthesis

Most oversight of gene synthesis currently takes place at the point of pur chase through commercial screening of DNA orders. The International Gene Synthesis Consortium (IGSC)—a biotechnology industry-led group of gene-synthesis companies and biosafety-oriented organizations that represents a majority of the global DNA synthesis market—has developed best practice screening protocols. 14 These protocols guide gene-synthesis companies in screening purchase requests against known pathogen samples and in reporting any anomalies that they detect. However, some providers still operate outside the IGSC framework and these protocols are not legally binding. Indeed, no country has implemented a comprehensive statutory DNA-screening requirement at the national level, and most existing guidance remains voluntary in practice, as with the United Kingdom’s ‘baseline

While some laboratories follow international standards such as ISO 35001—which sets out requirements for managing biological risks through structured policies, responsibilities and performance evaluation—others operate under informal or inconsistent biosafety guidelines, if any. 17 Broader implementation of ISO 35001 has been constrained by its voluntary status, the costs of certification, and the absence of alignment between the standard and most national regu latory frameworks. 18 The BWC’s CBM E nominally covers national legislation, regulations and other measures related to biological agents and toxins. However, its focus is on pathogens and toxins, rather than synthetic DNA or gene-synthesis activ ities. It does not request governments to indicate whether HCLs are conducting gene-synthesis research or procuring these services through commercial providers. This reflects the historical framing of the CBM E questionnaire, which was designed before such technologies emerged. More broadly, the CMB survey system as a whole does not fully capture how states oversee or procure synthetic DNA, leaving uncertainty as to whether these activities—often involving non-pathogenic genetic fragments—fall within its intended scope. Without additional reporting or clarification, there is limited insight into national governance of gene synthesis and the extent to which HCLs are engaging in these practices.

Several pilot efforts demonstrate how responsible research oversight can be reinforced by AI and by DLT—a class of digital systems, including blockchain, which store data across a decentralized network of computers, creating tamper-evident, timestamped records. **Use case 2. Using AI and DLT to strengthen digital data integrity and laboratory information-management systems**

Digital record-keeping has become central to high-containment research. It underpins experiment tracking, material inventories and biosafety documen tation. Laboratory information-management systems (LIMS) and electronic laboratory notebooks improve efficiency by standardizing documentation and reducing manual error.

##### Information management in high-containment laboratories

When applied within HCLs, LIMS introduce security challenges related to the loss, theft or manipulation of stored data. If data can be modified without detection or if access is not properly controlled, it becomes difficult to verify whether biosafety procedures were implemented correctly or whether bio logical materials were responsibly managed. Many high-containment facilities still rely on internal databases and track ing or incorporate mixed paper and digital records. This complicates

Digital records are typically held within institutions and are rarely available for external review, which limits their value for accountability or confidence- building. 21 Moreover, there is no global standard for data retention, validation or integrity checks across high-containment environments.

##### AI and DLT to manage information

Emerging technological advancements offer ways to bridge the account ability gap. Several commercial platforms have developed AI-enabled LIMS to standardize laboratory documentation. While many programs increas ingly advertise being ‘AI-ready’, new systems have embedded AI directly into the system architecture to enhance traditional LIMS functions or pro vide new, intelligent capabilities. AI can be used to analyse metadata from LIMS platforms to detect irregular data-access patterns or discrepancies in sample-handling logs, to flag potential insider misuse or to identify record inconsistencies. 22 DLT can be implemented to secure information in HCLs by incorporating user authentication, access controls and automated audit logs. This makes it harder to alter records without leaving a trace. These laboratory activity audit logs are inherently tamper-evident, as any attempt to alter or destroy a data set is recorded and linked to a specific user and time. 23

##### DLT for transparency in research

Large research institutes often manage extensive collections of pathogen samples that circulate between laboratories, sample respositories and stor age facilities. DLT can help to link physical transfers with digital records by creating verifiable chains of custody, thereby strengthening biosafety controls and auditability. 24 This technology can also link experiment results with specific protocols, storage conditions and user actions. While these sys tems are intended for operational efficiency rather than data security, they can reduce opportunities for data manipulation and create more transparent research environments. 25 However, these tools still function largely at the level of the individual facility, and their data outputs are not easily comparable across laboratories or national systems. This limits their use for broader transparency or assur ance purposes. In addition, balancing data traceability with protection of intellectual property and commercially sensitive information remains a key challenge in designing DLT systems for laboratory use. ##### Use case 3. Using DLT to enhance standards for pathogen repositories and material traceability

HCLs often maintain or use pathogen repositories that support development of and training and research on diagnostics and medical countermeasures. These well-characterized biological materials serve as reference strains, allowing laboratories around the world to calibrate testing and measurement methods and to validate the results against common standards. During outbreaks, repositories support rapid response by supplying verified samples for vaccine development and therapeutic testing. Centralized collections also reduce the need for laboratories to acquire materials independently from nature, which reduces biosafety risks and research duplication. These collections thus constitute essential scientific infrastructure.

##### Risk of pathogen misuse

There is no global standard for documenting the origin, movement or use of these pathogen samples; unlike, for instance, the movement of chemical 26 precursors controlled by the 1992 Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC). As a result, materials may be shared or transferred without full author ization or traceability, and in some cases they can be duplicated without clear accountability, creating uncertainty about their intended final use. Some of these risks can be mitigated through structured access systems such as the European Virus Archive Global (EVAg), which provides controlled distri bution of high-risk pathogens to verified research institutions under 27 standard ized biosafety and biosecurity conditions. In the United States, the BEI Resources repository distributes regulated biological materials to 28 approved institutions. Both repositories generally offer access while only charging users for shipping costs. Risk of misuse is compounded by ongoing disputes over international access and benefit-sharing (ABS) guidelines. This includes states’ differing interpretations of the 2010 Nagoya Protocol—a supplement to the 1992 Con vention on Biological Diversity that was designed to ensure that a country receives fair benefits when its biological resources are used for research or 29 commercial purposes. The protocol’s application to pathogens in public health emergencies remains contested by some states parties, impeding 30 cooperation and limiting transparency.

##### AI and DLT to manage materials

Emerging technologies could support better management of biological materials without requiring direct monitoring. Chain-of-custody systems using DLT can apply unique digital identifiers to track pathogens as they move between laboratories. DLT can also provide tamper-evident records of transfers, storage and sample destruction. Meanwhile, AI can be used to assist in analysing and reconciling inventory data to pinpoint anomalies pos sibly linked to unauthorized access or movement. Some pilot initiatives have demonstrated these possibilities and a proof-of-concept study was documented in 2023 using IBM’s blockchain 31 platform. The International Air Transport Association (IATA), an airline trade association, has piloted blockchain-based tracking for shipping pharmaceutical and temperature-sensitive goods, which may offer 32 transferable lessons for the global traceability of pathogen material. In the European Union (EU), the TRACE project has piloted digital provenance tools for tracking the origin and transfers of samples, with results expected at 33 the project’s conclusion in 2026. Extending such mechanisms could help strengthen implementation of articles III and IV of the BWC by improving accountability in how high-risk biological materials are managed and exchanged.

#### III. The way forward

The emerging technologies explored in the three use cases have largely been developed and deployed at the facility level; however, governments can incentivize or standardize their use through national biosafety frameworks and reporting formats. Consistent adoption of such practices can indirectly enhance states’ internal capacity to monitor laboratory activities reliably and document biosafety practices consistently. By strengthening national oversight infrastructure, technologies such as DLT traceability platforms, standardized reporting systems and AI-enabled documentation can help states parties meet their BWC obligations more effectively while building confidence in peaceful research intent. When integrated into transparency mechanisms such as CBMs, such systems could improve information quality and support technical cooperation among states parties. Addressing disparities in capacity, cost and access will require not only institutional innovation but also practical incentives for participation. For low- and middle-income countries, strengthening transparency and responsible stewardship can demonstrate commitment to BWC Article X (on international cooperation). It can also contribute to a more cooperative environment in which confidence-building, technical exchange and capacity development are shared priorities among all states parties.
