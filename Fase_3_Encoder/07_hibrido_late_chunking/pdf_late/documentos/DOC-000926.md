### Introduction

Nearly seven decades after the launch of the first human-made satellite, Sputnik-1, 1 global spaceflight has reached an inflection point. In 2024, a historic milestone was surpassed: 254 successful rocket launches took place in a single year; 32 Member States submitted registration information on 2,429 functional space objects; and 730 notifications of space object re-entries were submitted to the Secretary-General. These milestones reflect more than just growth – they mark a dramatic acceleration in space activities, driven by reduced barriers to entry and a rapidly expanding global space sector.

This sharp rise reflects a broader shift in the scale and accessibility of space activity. While many launches support the deployment of large and mega-satellite constellations, others signal the growing participation of governmental, civil and commercial actors – both new and established – operating in Earth orbit and beyond.

As the orbital environment becomes increasingly crowded, the need for transparency over the jurisdiction and control of space objects has never been more important, and this is where registration comes in.

Since 1962, Member States have registered their space objects with the United Nations Secretary-General – initially on a voluntary basis under General Assembly resolution 1721 B (XVI) (1961), and later as a legal obligation for State Parties under the Convention on Registration of Objects Launched into Outer Space.

To date, States and international intergovernmental organizations have registered approximately 19,928 functional space objects, including satellites, probes, landers, rovers, cargo craft, crewed spacecraft and space stations. 2

## Toolkit on Good Registration Practices for Objects Launched into Outer Space

This Registration Toolkit is a practical resource to empower States to fulfil their international obligations related to the registration of objects launched into outer space, with the objective of enhancing international expertise, the current rate of submission of registration information to the United Nations and the transparency of space activities. The toolkit guides nations through the process of establishing and maintaining national registration frameworks, thereby implementing the international regulatory framework on registration at the national level, and of submitting notifications to the United Nations Register of Objects Launched into Outer Space, which the United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA) maintains on behalf of the Secretary-General.

Targeted at all Member States of the United Nations, this toolkit consists of examples of good practices, national implementation case studies, legal background and practical checklists, making the guidance both accessible and action oriented.

The methodology of the toolkit draws on the work by UNOOSA on the Registration Project, including interviews with national stakeholders, expert consultations and capacity-building workshops.

This research underpins the toolkit’s evidence-based recommendations and reflects a wide range of national and regional perspectives and good practices, taken from interviews with national stakeholders, a Stakeholder Study (ST/SPACE/91) developed under Phase One of the Registration Project, two meetings of National Focal Points on Registration, an analysis of diverse national registration frameworks and international capacity-building events co-organized by UNOOSA and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU).

### 1. International space law and registration

The first instrument to call on States to provide information on objects launched into outer space is General Assembly resolution 1721 B (XVI) adopted on 20 December 1961. The Treaty on Principles Governing the Activities of States in the Exploration and Use of Outer Space, including the Moon and Other Celestial Bodies (the Outer Space Treaty) (1967) went further and set out under article VIII, that a State Party to the Treaty on whose registry an object launched into outer space is carried retains jurisdiction and control of that object.

The obligations set out in the Outer Space Treaty were expanded upon through subsequent treaties, and with respect to registration, in the Convention on Registration of Objects Launched into Outer Space (Registration Convention). The Convention was adopted on 12 November 1974 in General Assembly resolution 3235 (XXIX), opened for signature on 14 January 1975 and entered into force on 15 September 1976.

While the Registration Convention is legally binding on States Parties to the Convention, registration practices that have evolved through its implementation may also be relevant for non-State Parties to the Registration Convention, who can submit information on space objects launched into outer space via General Assembly resolu tion 1721 B (XVI) to the United Nations. In chapter 2 on good registration practices, the toolkit addresses both States Parties and non-State Parties (United Nations Member States) to the Convention.

In practice, this means that the Registration Convention and General Assembly resolution 1721 B (XVI) are alterna tive vehicles for States Parties to the Convention, or respectively for Member States of the United Nations who are not Party to the Convention, to submit information on the registration of their space objects to the United Nations.

General Assembly resolution 1721 B calls upon Member States that are launching objects into outer space to furnish information on those objects to the Secretary-General to be published in – what has evolved to be – the United Nations Register of Objects Launched into Outer Space.

The Registration Convention requires the State of registry to establish and maintain an appropriate registry (arti cle II). Where there are two or more launching States, it requests launching States to jointly determine which of them would register an object (article II); and sets out the information that States of registry should provide to the Secretary-General (article IV).

A critical step for States is to analyse their existing national framework and identify the necessary elements to implement their international registration obligations effectively. This can be undertaken with a view to the following criteria:

- **** Role of national laws and regulations
- **** Examining how national legislation implements and enforces the international obligations derived from the Registration Convention and other relevant instruments. - **** Designation of a national authority for the registration of space objects
- **** The process of officially identifying and empowering a specific national body (e.g., a space agency, a ministry of foreign affairs, or a dedicated regulatory office) to establish and maintain the national registry for objects launched into outer space. - **** Defining national data requirements
- **** Beyond the information required by article IV of the Registration Convention, States may decide to require additional data from operators for their national registries. These may include: – Detailed technical specifications of the object – Operator details (commercial entities, academic institutions) – Insurance information – Expected operational lifetime and end-of-life disposal plans (e.g., de-orbiting strategy) – Payload information (where not classified)
- **** Such information does not necessarily have to be collected specifically for registration purposes but can be part of the (broader) information required from national entities when applying for authorization, if required under national law
- **** Assessing existing capabilities and gaps
- **** Conducting an internal review to identify current capacities for data collection, storage and reporting, as well as any legislative, technical or human resource gaps that need to be addressed

## Objectives of space object registration

Effective space object registration is guided by the following objectives, which benefit States as well as the inter national community:

- **** Transparency:
*Objective:* To ensure that information on space objects is made publicly available in a timely and accessible manner, fostering trust and predictability in space activities

- **** Accountability:
*Objective:* To clearly identify the State carrying a space object on its registry, thereby facilitating the attribution of liability for any damage caused, as per the 1972 Liability Convention

- **** Safety and sustainability:
*Objective:* To contribute to the safety of space operations and the long-term sustainability of the space environment by providing essential data for space situational awareness (SSA), collision avoidance and debris mitigation efforts

- **** Comprehensiveness and accuracy:
*Objective:* To ensure that registration data are complete, accurate and regularly updated throughout a space object’s lifecycle, from launch to de-orbit

- **** Adaptability:
*Objective:* To develop national registration mechanisms that can adapt to rapid technological advancements and evolving operational paradigms in the space sector

By ratifying the Registration Convention and having national legislation in place, States can assist the creation of a predictable regulatory environment for investors and can promote the growth of private sector activities.

## 2.1 ESTABLISH: the national registry for objects launched into outer space

ESTABLISH focuses on the foundational steps involved in setting up a functional national registry for objects launched into outer space, serving as the primary record for these objects for the State concerned. It addresses the establishment, criteria and content of a registry for objects launched into outer space as well as the definition of space objects at the national level and relevant aspects of national space legislation.

### Importance of establishing a registry at the national level

As determined in international instruments on the registration of objects launched into outer space, the act of “registration” of an object launched into outer space is its entry in a registry at the national level. 4 Registration of objects launched into outer space is therefore an act within the domestic domain of a State and the moment of registration of a space object is the moment that it is entered into the (national) registry of objects launched into outer space, where the State is the State of registry for the said object. ### Establishment of a national registry as a State Party and non-State Party to the Registration Convention

In short, States do not have to be State Parties to the Registration Convention in order to establish a registry of objects launched into outer space at the national level. While the international obligation to establish a national registry applies to States Parties to the Convention, in countries that have not (yet) ratified the Convention, a national registry may be, and regularly is, established without an existing international obligation to do so. This falls within the purview of domestic legislation.

***Good practice:*** non-State Parties to the Registration Convention establish a national registry even without an existing international obligation to do so

### Formal establishment of a registry on objects launched into outer space

States are at liberty to opt for a formal establishment of their (national) registries through legislative acts at the national level. While there is no international legal obligation for a formal anchorage in the national legislative framework and countries may establish ad-hoc, informal registries, State practice shows that national registries are commonly established through formal domestic legislation, which may for instance take the form of the adoption of specific legislation or the enactment of executive decrees. Moreover, while most States create self-standing registries, States may extend their civil aviation registries to encompass space activities. 5 For States Parties to the Registration Convention, the (national) space object registry is, in most cases, established in reference to the international legal obligation in article II of the Convention.

***Good practice:*** formally establish the (national) registry through domestic legislative acts

### National space legislation and the establishment of a registry

In short, States do not have to have domestic legislation in place in order to establish a registry of objects launched into outer space. However, registration of objects launched into outer space may be, and often is, included in a broader regulatory framework addressing space activities domestically. Motivating factors to establish national legislation include creating a robust and predictable regulatory environment to attract investors and strengthen the domestic space economy.

Several States currently are or have recently been in the process of updating or revising their national space legislation.

Currently, a growing number of States enact or revise domestic space legislation and/or regulations to govern the conduct of space activities at the national level to accommodate changes within their national space sector. Often, this is triggered by a shift away from space activities being conducted solely by the government, for example, due to the increased role of the private sector or academia. This is often done by addressing the seven key elements of international space law, namely: scope of application of the law; authorization and licensing of activities of non-governmental entities; continuing supervision of activities of non-governmental entities; the registration of space objects; liability and insurance; safety; and transfer of ownership. These have been set out by General Assembly resolution 68/74 of 11 December 2013 entitled Recommendations on national legislation relevant to the peaceful exploration and use of outer space. Examples of national space law including references to the seven key elements of international space law can be found on the UNOOSA ASTRO Portal.

As for the formal dimension of national space legislation, some States opt for a single piece of legislation while others adopt separate laws and regulations applicable to different types of space activities (such as, communication or remote sensing). Formally, space legislation can encompass various instruments of national legislation, such as legal acts and decrees.

### Reporting the establishment of a registry to the United Nations

States Parties to the Registration Convention submit the successful establishment of a national registry to the Secretary-General under article II of the Convention. This is done by official communication channels of States to the United Nations and the communication is to be directed to UNOOSA, as the United Nations entity that discharges the obligations of the Secretary-General with respect to the United Nations Register of Objects Launched onto Outer Space. UNOOSA provides an index of notifications by Member States and organizations on the establishment of national registries of objects launched into outer space.

Space objects to be recorded in the (national) registry are referred to throughout the international regulatory framework for space activities, and the Registration Convention provides that the term “space object” includes component parts of a space object as well as its launch vehicle and parts thereof (article I (b)).

##### International instruments. Registration Convention: article I (b)

***Article I***

(b) The term “space object” includes component parts of a space object as well as its launch vehicle and parts thereof. *Registration Convention: Article I, paragraph (b)*
While the term “space object” can have various definitions at the domestic level, reference to “objects launched into outer space” at the international level refers to the plurality of objects launched independent of whether they fall within the respective domestic definitions of space objects.

Most States with domestic space legislation define the term “space object” in their domestic legal context and do so in conformity with the definitions used in the United Nations treaties governing outer space activities.

***Good practice:*** define “space object” in national legislation in conformity with the international framework governing space activities including article I (b) of the Registration Convention

Other than the stipulation in the international regulatory framework, it is under the purview of States to define space objects domestically. It is good practice in domestic definitions to apply the term to artificial or human-made objects. In addition, several States have included references to manned or unmanned objects.

***Good practice:*** define “space object” including artificial or human- made objects

Most national legislation and good practices use “space object” to refer to objects launched into outer space, meaning to either Earth orbit or beyond. In some cases, domestic definitions refer to both orbital and suborbital trajectories.

A commendable practice, as stipulated in article I (b) of the Registration Convention, is the registration of launch vehicle components, including rocket bodies (also referred to as rocket stages) – even when these components are not intended for long-term operation or functionality in orbit. Several States have adopted the practice of registering rocket bodies, showcasing their commitment to responsible space conduct and adherence to the principles of long-term sustainability.

***Good practice:*** include launch vehicles in the domestic definition of space object and registration practice

While some States specify that a space object performs a military or governmental function, other States provide generic descriptions of the function when submitting registration information to the United Nations. State practice shows that major space nations routinely register such satellites.

Keeping consistent recorded information on space objects is another important aspect regarding the determination of which classes of objects are entered into the registry at the domestic level. This concerns both the information recorded in the (national) registry as well as in the registration information submitted to the United Nations and spans across mission types such as military, civil or academic missions.

### Supplementary and complementary registries

The international framework on the registration of objects launched into outer space does not set a limit as to how many registries may be set up domestically for objects launched into outer space.

As national registries established pursuant to article II of the Registration Convention are meant to contain space objects of States of registry, some States – in addition to their (primary) registry – have established additional registries for space objects for which they are not the State of registry but have some degree of involvement. These registries are commonly referred to as “supplementary” or “complementary” registries.

This applies especially in cases where States have operational spaceports and where they may not be the State of registry for a space object but, for example, may wish to keep track of objects launched from their territory. In other instances, additional registries are established where respective entities under the jurisdiction of a State operate a space object, but the State is not a launching State as defined in the Registration Convention. Those space objects may then be recorded in a supplementary or complementary registry. Another example of the need for a supplementary or complementary registry could be to ensure that transfers of ownership can be effectively monitored: for example, when country A sells a satellite to a company in country B but the satellite may still be operated by country A. In this instance, country A could record the satellite in a supplementary registry. 6

##### SPOTLIGHT

##### United Kingdom Supplementary Space Object Registry

The U nited Kingdom Supplementary Registry includes different categories of space object associ- ated with the U nited Kingdom. The first category includes space objects for which the U nited Kingdom was the launching (and supervising) State but have now been transferred to another jurisdiction. The second category covers objects for which the U nited Kingdom has issued licences under U nited Kingdom law, but where another State is agreed or deemed to be the primary launching and super- vising State under international law. Objects may appear on the U nited Kingdom Registry, and on another State’s domestic registry. ### Non-governmental space activities

Governmental space activities have been carried out since the beginning of the space age, with non-governmental activities increasing their presence in national space economies globally over the last decades. In some States, challenges have arisen from the emergence of non-governmental space actors. Often, this leads to the initiation of a process to update registration-related regulations and procedures and can include proactive engagement with the national non-governmental space sector to seek information on the laws and regulations of other countries.

Submission by private sector entities of relevant registration information on space objects to the national competent entity is often incentivized by registration being seen to increase prestige, publicity and the potential for further commercial opportunities that come from successful launches and adherence to regulation.

Moreover, with regard to non-governmentally owned or operated space objects, the operator is listed in the national registry by the majority of States. State practices vary as to the publication of the identity of the owner/operator: while many States publish this information, some States opt to withhold information on the identity of the owner/ operator from the publicly available content of the registry.

Many States consider such “overarching” or “blanket” arrangements, reviewed periodically, as a useful mechanism to provide clarity on which State will enter the object in their national registry and have the certainty the space object would not remain unregistered.

### Determining the State of registry

Among the launching States of an object launched into outer space, if there is more than one, one State will be determined as the State of registry. The State of registry will be the State entering the object in its (national) registry and submitting the respective registration information to the United Nations. Factors that play a role in the determination of State of registry by States include ownership, interest in a space object, the authorization or licensing of the launch or the operation of the space object, the involvement of citizens or national entities (public or private) in the space activity as well as operating or having use of the space object.

In the case that States are neither Party to the Outer Space Treaty nor to the Registration Convention, some States are willing to voluntarily accept the obligations even when they are non-State Parties. It is important to note that the absence of international obligations does not prevent Member States from registering space objects domestically and submitting registration information on their satellites with the United Nations by way of General Assembly resolution 1721 B (XVI). This can ensure that a space object does not remain unregistered.

### Multinational entities

There can be scenarios or instances where determining the State of registry becomes challenging, especially if operational control lies with a multinational entity, at which point consideration would include not only determin ing which State had operational control but also legal control. Such complex arrangements are typically negotiated between the respective States. For example, some States require in their space laws for multinational entities to seek authorization before they are able to launch from their territory, and for some this includes having designated the State of registry prior to launch.

### Large constellations and mega-constellations

When a State authorizes the launch of thousands of small satellites, for instance, for a global Internet constellation, challenges emerge concerning how to manage the sheer volume of registration data, ensure timely updates, and provide meaningful information to the international community without overwhelming the system. Possible approaches involve batch registration, 11 simplified initial reporting followed by detailed updates for specific subsets, or national regulations requiring constellation-level summaries in addition to individual object data.

### Transfer of ownership and international cooperation

Continuous evolution of the concept of State of registry is necessary as space activities and technology evolve. The transfer of ownership of a space object in orbit is a recent phenomenon, emerging in the wake of the increasing privatization and commercialization of space activities, which is not specifically addressed in the five United Nations treaties on outer space and which may lead to a change in the State of registry.

When a space object is sold (ownership) or leased (control) or ownership is transferred through other arrange ments to an entity in another State while in orbit, the question presents itself as to how the changes in ownership or operational control should be reflected in the national registries of the States concerned, as there may be implications for jurisdiction and control over the space object. ### Deployment of space objects from space stations in orbit

The deployment of space objects from space stations, commonly small satellites released from onboard deployers, has become a routine aspect of contemporary orbital operations. Most such deployments involve CubeSats or other small payloads delivered to low earth orbit aboard a cargo vehicle, then released from the space station at a later date, leading to a time lag between launch and deployment. In these cases, often, States submit the date of deployment – not the date of launch from Earth – for purposes of registration and cataloguing.

Examples of deployment platforms include the Japanese Kibo module on the International Space Station (ISS) and the Nanoracks CubeSat Deployer operated by commercial providers via the ISS United States segment.

### International cooperation and other forms of information-sharing with the United Nations

Article XI of the Outer Space Treaty complements registration obligations by encouraging States to share informa tion about the nature, conduct, locations and results of their space activities, especially those with the potential to affect other States. In practice, this provision supports and extends the purpose of registration by encouraging States to provide advance notice of missions involving, for example, nuclear power sources or remote sensing, and to report unusual incidents or outcomes. Though used to various extents by States, article XI constitutes a key tool for fostering international trust and accountability in space operations.

UNOOSA hosts a page with an Index of Submissions by States under Article XI of the Outer Space Treaty on its website. Moreover, the Working Group on the Status and Application of the Five United Nations Treaties on Outer Space under the Legal Subcommittee of COPUOS currently undertakes work on an exchange of views on the implementation of article XI of the Outer Space Treaty.
