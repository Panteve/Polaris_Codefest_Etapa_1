## DEBATE OVER MISSION AND RATIONALE FOR THE CHINESE REUSABLE EXPERIMENTAL SPACECRAFT

China’s official objectives of the program include verification and space science experiments to “provide technical support for the peaceful use of space” and to “pave the way for more convenient and affordable round-trip methods for the peaceful use of space in the future.” 6 However, many have questioned and speculated upon its official mission.

Over the course of its four missions, the CSSHQ has tested multiple technologies, including vertical takeoff and horizontal landing, capture and docking operations, and rendezvous and formation flying. The first CSSHQ mission lasted from September 4 to September 6, 2020, during which time an unknown object was deployed and subsequently catalogued by the US military (Object A, 2020-063G). 7 The object, which was believed to be a subsatellite, broadcast S-band transmissions for several weeks after the spaceplane’s mission concluded. 8 The second CSSHQ mission began on August 4, 2022 and concluded on May 8, 2023, totaling 276 days in orbit. On October 23, 2022, the CSSHQ raised its perigee⁹ and released a new object catalogued by the US military as OBJECT J (2022-093J). 10 The CSSHQ is believed to have conducted rendezvous and proximity operations on multiple occasions, as well as at least two capture/ docking operations with Object J in November-December 2022, January 2023, and February-March 2023. On at least five occasions, Object J maneuvered independently of the spaceplane, indicating that the subsatellite was equipped with independent propulsive capabilities. 11 The third CSSHQ mission began on December 14, 2023 and concluded on September 5, 2024, totaling 268 days in orbit. In late January 2024, CSSHQ raised its perigee, and on May 24, 2024, it released an unknown object (OBJECT G, 2023- 195G) into orbit. After releasing Object G, CSSHQ performed a series of close maneuvers with the object and possible recapture operations¹² on June 5-7, June 11-12, June 14, and June 15-16, 2024. 13 Following the third CSSHQ mission, Chinese press reporting emphasized the success of the experimental mission and the growing maturity of the spaceplane program. 14 A fourth flight of the CSSHQ started in February 2026 with a launch from Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center via a Long March-2F (PRC Test Spacecraft 4, 2026-024A). 15 The spaceplane was first catalogued to be in an orbit of 50° of 344 km x 590 km; about five days after its launch, it maneuvered to a circular 588 km x 597 km orbit. 16 According to state news agency *Xinhua*, the spaceplane “will conduct technological verification for reusable spacecraft, providing technical support for the peaceful use of space.” 17

There is speculation that the CSSHQ could serve as a space weapon launch platform or a platform for surveillance, intelligence, and early-warning missions. China’s *Global Times* outlet refuted these claims, reiterating that the system is “just a verification platform for testing the reentry glide return technology of a winged aircraft.” The *Global Times* article was subsequently featured on the official website of the China Academy of Space Technology (CAST). 18 As of May 2026, the CSSHQ has not approached nor maneuvered with non-Chinese space objects. Similar to the X-37B spaceplane, the CSSHQ’s utility as a space-to-ground weapon is constrained by orbital mechanics. Any hyperkinetic weapon dropped from the spaceplane’s bay would need to be equipped with its own thrusters capable of performing a deorbit burn. Based on the estimated size of the spaceplane, such a weapon would be unlikely to fit within the spaceplane’s payload bay. 19 However, co-orbital concerns remain around the CSSHQ’s dual use capabilities, due to the spaceplane’s demonstrated ability to release, dock, and maneuver with objects in low Earth orbit.

## CHINESE REUSABLE EXPERIMENTAL SPACECRAFT FEATURES

Similar to the X-37B, the CSSHQ is uncrewed and appears to be relatively small. 20 China’s use of the Long March 2F rocket for the first four flights suggests that the CSSHQ is comparatively larger in size than China’s Shenlong spaceplane model released in 2000, due to the Long March 2F rocket’s reported payload capacity of around 8 metric tons for low Earth orbit placement. 21 The CSSHQ spaceplane is thought to return to Earth autonomously through a horizontal runway landing, similar to a conventional aircraft.

The CSSHQ’s time spent in orbit increased significantly between its first flight, which lasted two days, and its second flight, which lasted 276 days. The time intervals between flights has decreased, from nearly two years between the first and second flight to seven months between the second and third flight. 22

## MANEUVERABILITY OF THE CHINESE REUSABLE EXPERIMENTAL SPACECRAFT

During its second flight, the CSSHQ raised its orbit significantly, from 346 km by 593 km to 607 km by 597 km, rising to a nearly circular orbit. During the third CSSHQ flight, the spaceplane raised its orbit from 333 km by 348 km to 609 km by 601 km. 23 As stated above, the CSSHQ has demonstrated an ability to perform maneuvers and capture/docking operations with other objects equipped with independent propulsive systems. These maneuvers and orbit raises suggest that the CSSHQ possesses significant delta-v capability.

## TRANSPARENCY CONCERNS

Much of the speculation over the Chinese Reusable Experimental Spacecraft stems from the program’s secretive nature. China has not revealed many official details about the spaceplane’s mission or on- orbit activities. Official statements are limited to launch announcements, which are released day-of, and applause for the program’s success. 24 China has repeatedly claimed that the Chinese Reusable Experimental Spacecraft program is in line with the country’s commitment to the “peaceful uses of outer space,” but China has not registered the spaceplane nor its sub satellites with the United Nations for any of its past four missions, in contradiction to its legal responsibilities under the 1975 Registration Convention. 25

## DEPLOYMENT OF SATELLITES

The Chinese Reusable Experimental Spacecraft has deployed satellites during each of the spaceplane’s first three missions, although China has never announced or confirmed these deployments. Identification and announcements of deployed objects have been conducted by commercial SSA companies, and object cataloging has been done by the US military. The satellite deployed during the first mission demonstrated transmission broadcast capabilities, 26 and the satellite deployed during its second mission is thought to have displayed independent propulsive capabilities. The CSSHQ has conducted numerous close maneuvers and capture/docking operations with the deployed satellites.
