#### I. Introduction

Across the Sahel—including Burkina Faso, Chad, Mali, Mauritania and Niger—multiple global crises are converging in unprecedented ways, escalating humanitarian needs, reversing development gains and diminishing prospects for peace. Overcoming these crises is a critical problem facing 1 local populations and the international community. Although official development assistance (ODA) reached a record high in 2023, aid allocation remains severely misaligned with peacebuilding needs, particularly in highly fragile regions such as the Sahel. ODA allocated to countries in highly fragile contexts fell to a 20-year low in 2022–23, even as armed conflict in the Sahel intensified. Funding for conflict prevention was particularly scarce, making up less than 4 per cent of total ODA—its second lowest share since 2004. Meanwhile, humanitarian ODA reached record highs, reflecting a continued emphasis on crisis response over addressing root causes of fragility. 2 This misalignment undermines resilience across affected contexts. The growing politicization of humanitarian aid exacerbates this misalignment. In conflict zones, aid is increasingly instrumentalized to serve political or military agendas. Amid intense great power rivalry and 3 weak international structures for managing crisis, funding decisions are more heavily influenced by political considerations, geostrategic priorities and security cooperation. In the Sahel this situation is compounded by 4 increasing resistance from both governments and civilians to Western presence, which they perceive as an extension of foreign influence and dominance, in particularly from France. 5

Given these political and operational difficulties, addressing fragility in such complex, protracted crises demands an integrated approach across the humanitarian–development–peace (HDP) nexus. This means increasing and sustaining development programming in crisis situations and sharing responsibility among HDP actors to avoid undermining one another. It also requires a commitment by humanitarian agencies to consider how their efforts can support longer-term goals and facilitate progress towards sustainable peace. 6 Finally, it requires funding tools that are both fast and flexible and are capable of delivering immediate results, while supporting long-term impact over several years. The Sahel Resilience Partnership (SRP) is a concrete example of this approach that began its inception phase in January 2024 and was officially launched in July 2024. It aims to reduce long-term vulnerability and enhance resilience. The SRP relies on the United Nations definition of resilience, which emphasizes the ability to withstand and adapt to risks while safeguarding the foundations for peace and sustainable development. 7 Funded by the German Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development (Bundesministerium für wirtschaftliche Zusammenarbeit und Entwicklung, BMZ), the SRP brings together the Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit (GIZ), which is Germany’s international develop ment agency, the UN Children’s Fund (UNICEF) and the World Food Programme (WFP). By leveraging their complementary humanitarian and development mandates and strengths (see table 1), the SRP aims to operationalize the HDP nexus through coordinated, multisectoral programming. #### II. Humanitarian–development–peace nexus approaches in the Sahel: Why they matter

HDP nexus approaches are based on the premise that humanitarian, development and peacebuilding efforts must be coordinated, coherent and complementary. 11 By building on the respective comparative strengths of humanitarian actors, development actors and peacebuilding actors operating in the same locations, these approaches are designed to adapt flexibly to rapidly evolving contexts. 12 Simultaneously, they aim to reduce vulner address the root causes of conflict, strengthen risk-management capacities ability, and improve overall aid effectiveness. 13 The nexus embodies the vision of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals and the 2018 report by the UN secretary-general on sustaining peace. 14 These frameworks recognize that sustainable development cannot be achieved without peace, and that peace cannot endure without sustainable development. At the same time, they underscore the urgent need to address conflict in order to alleviate human suffering and reduce the scale and frequency of humanitarian emergencies. Meeting immediate humanitarian needs can create opportunities for long-term development. Development initiatives, in turn, help strengthen resilience if simultaneous and aligned peacebuilding efforts address the root causes of instability and create a more stable environment for develop ment to solidify. 15 However, incorporating peacebuilding into the work of humanitarian and development actors has proven to be hard. There is no commonly agreed-upon conceptual definition of the peacebuilding aspect of the nexus; instead, the mandates of each of the actors that address conflict shape how they can work on peace. Humanitarian and development actors that operationalize the peacebuilding component of the HDP nexus tend to focus on fostering long-term, transformative change within systems and societies. This typic ally involves strengthening social cohesion, human rights and justice through conflict-sensitive bottom-up action and relationship-building at the individual and community levels. This is frequently referred to as small-p peacebuilding. 16 These approaches contrast with top-down peace negotiations at the national level, which is commonly referred to as

Although distinguishing between small-p and big-P peacebuilding can be administratively and operationally convenient for donors and implementing agencies, framing them as separate or discrete categories risks oversimplifi cation. Such a recognition can foster coherence across actors and levels of intervention. Against these challenges, putting HDP approaches into practice and achieving the expected positive feedback loop requires, among other things, conflict sensitivity, prioritization of local participation and owner ship, operating in the same geographical areas (geographical convergence), coordination, complementarity of partners with distinct mandates and expertise, and strategic alignment of programmes and interventions (programmatic convergence). 19 In the Sahel, where the linkages between multiple drivers of fragility and insecurity mutually exacerbate each other, HDP nexus partnerships have greater potential than siloed interventions. #### III. The Sahel Resilience Partnership: Strengths and challenges

The Sahel Resilience Partnership aligns GIZ’s Sahel Resilience Strengthening (SARES) project, UNICEF’s Building Resilience in the Sahel (BRS) programme and the WFP’s Sahel Integrated Resilience Programme (SIRP). 38 The purpose of joining the resilience efforts of GIZ, UNICEF and the WFP together in a portfolio of interventions is to combine their respect ive strengths in a coherent, complementary and coordinated fashion. This is expected to enhance impact across four common outcomes, linked to the main drivers of fragility in the Sahel: food and nutrition security; climate- resilient ecosystems; continuous, shock-resilient social services; and conflict resolution and social cohesion (see table 4). 39 Food and nutrition interventions (e.g. food and cash transfers and treatment of acute malnutrition) address immediate humanitarian needs. Climate and social service interventions (e.g. rehabilitation of degraded land, climate-resilient water, sanitation and hygiene, and school meal programming) complement immediate food and nutrition needs to create long-term resilience. Conflict-resolution and social cohesion activities (e.g. intercommunal dialogues, cultural activities, and improved use and ownership rights of restored land) aim to improve the environment for resilience-building interventions to take root. ##### Design and implementation strengths

Analysis of the set-up of the SRP reveals four key design and implemen tation strengths: its multilevel governance structure to align the resilience activities of GIZ, UNICEF and the WFP; an adaptable joint regional plan; common community engagement and participatory planning; and in-built knowledge generation. Each is examined in turn below.

##### Adaptable joint regional plan

##### Inter-agency coordination

Inter-agency coordination is laborious and complicated. The three partners work in separate offices with budgets whose sizes and cycles differ 71 (see table 6). GIZ operates with a project logic and dedicated leadership and staffing structure from the regional to the country level for the duration of SARES. In contrast, UNICEF and the WFP take a programmatic, country- based approach with regional and thematic advisors who work across 72 different interventions and with frequent staff changes. Incoming staff may 73 be unfamiliar with the strategic complementarity pursued. UNICEF and the WFP are GIZ’s main partners, while GIZ is one of many 74 part ners for UNICEF’s and the WFP’s resilience programming. UNICEF staff have sometimes prioritized meetings with government authorities over SRP coordination meetings, given their focus on strengthening social 75 protection systems and services. Partly due to their different mandates, the organizations also work at different speeds. Interviewees reported that humanitarian actors (e.g. the WFP) tend to focus on being fast, while development actors (e.g. GIZ) 76 implement with longer time frames in mind. Partners need to strike a balance between coordination, planning and implementation across agencies.

##### Spotlight on social cohesion

Integrating the peace component across interventions has historically been difficult given constraints on mandates, concerns about humanitarian neutrality, different understandings of peace across organizations and a lack of measurement frameworks. 95 Building on earlier resilience-building collabor streaming the building of social cohesion across GIZ, UNICEF and WFP ations, the SRP seeks to promote peace and reduce conflict by main interventions, rather than treating it as a standalone effort. 96 In practice, this means designing both implementation processes and technical interventions intentionally to promote social cohesion and prevent conflict. Implementation processes describe the ways in which activities should be carried out. For example, they ensure that planning, delivery and engagement are inclusive, participatory and equitable. These approaches support social cohesion by modelling continuous inclusion and partic ipation, particularly of marginalized groups such as women and youth and disabled or displaced people. Technical interventions, in turn, focus on the content of programming and are designed to foster positive inter across groups and with government authorities. For example, inclusive actions social protection programmes can build trust in institutions, while natural resource-management initiatives can incorporate conflict prevention and can strengthen legitimate, collaborative governance. 97 This combination of participatory implementation processes and technical interventions corresponds to complex linkages between social, political, eco nomic and environmental drivers of social tension across the Sahel. For example, social cohesion in Mauritania is threatened by ethno-racial divisions and uneven access to basic services and infrastructure throughout its vast territory. 98 Historically marginalized ethno-racial groups and impoverished rural populations face the additional impacts of climate change, eco nomic shocks and migration pressure. 99 Climate change is disrupting transhumance periods in Niger, driving conflict over resources and land rights between farmers and pastoralists. 100 These tensions are compounded by political and economic shocks following from changes in governance and post-coup sanctions since 2023. 101 These examples demonstrate the need for complementary, inclusive and conflict-sensitive programming. The SRP aims to promote social cohesion through conflict-sensitive inclusive, participatory, bottom-up design and equitable access to services. #### IV. Conclusions and ways forward

Several good practices and lessons for deepening collaboration and increasing efficiency emerge from the SRP experience of operationalizing the HDP nexus. They include the following:

1. Breaking down siloed organizational working approaches through donor insistence on strategic complementarity of interventions that translates into the implementation of partnership activities. This requires leadership buy-in.
2. Establishing a clear steering structure with partnership focal points at all levels to ensure ongoing partner engagement, joint coordination, strategizing and implementation, as well as inter- agency learning.
3. Generating an adaptable common operational plan that defines joint outcomes and how to reach them with a division of labour based on organizational strengths and complementarity. The SRP did this through an inception period during which partners learned about organizational differences, strengths and constraints.
4. Engaging continuously with communities through ongoing joint partner processes focused on strengthening community ownership, participation and inclusion.
5. Conducting regular joint field missions and working through connected or common implementing partners to strengthen strategic complementarity and efficiency.
6. Embedding knowledge generation and learning into implementation through local and international research partnerships.

Future recommendations for the SRP and organizations seeking to operationalize the HDP nexus include the following.

1. Avoiding competition for protagonism and deepening accountability towards each other, in addition to the affected populations and the donor.
2. Seeking operational convergence at village level for deeper complementarity. This may involve a trade-off between breadth and depth of coverage.
3. Deepening operational and programmatic complementarity and ensuring staff and collaborators understand what the SRP is about. This could involve strengthened knowledge management within and across organizations to overcome the challenges related to frequent staff rotation.
4. Strengthening community ownership further by sourcing solutions from communities.
5. Generating specific opportunities for marginalized groups to elevate their position within their communities.
6. Continuing to identify entry points for everyday peacebuilding, for example, through sustained community engagement and reliable social service provision with a focus on prevention.
7. Sharing and systematically comparing available data more proactively, harmonizing selected indicators for joint assessments and improving data-management tools to avoid overlap. This may include consideration of a common monitoring and evaluation approach to investigate the theory of change underlying the SRP or use of common surveys to measure trust, respect, tolerance, solidarity and perceptions of equal opportunities.
8. Tracking where and how the SRP managed to avoid duplication and recorded efficiency gains, for example, through joint field missions or shared contractors.
9. Nurturing a culture of cooperation between HDP actors beyond the SRP.
