#### South Korea would have much to lose by pursuing a nuclear

#### weapons program, a hypothetical idea that its president raised

#### recently. It would entail Seoul withdrawing from the Nuclear

#### Non-Proliferation Treaty, triggering a cascade of negative

#### consequences. For Russia, that would effectively halt bilateral

#### co-operation on peaceful uses of nuclear energy.

#### It’s time for international stakeholders to renew efforts

#### to reduce tensions on the Korean Peninsula and create a

#### sustainable regional security architecture with the long-term

#### goal to free the entire peninsula from nuclear weapons, write

#### Vladislav Chernavskikh and Anton Khlopkov.

In Ja nua ry this year, South Korean President Yoon Suk-yeol said that due to the “growing threat” from N orth Korea, Seoul might consider deploy- ing US tactical nuclear weapons in the country or pursuing its own military nuclear program. 1 T he latter openly contradicts South Korea’s obli- gations under the T reaty on the N 2 on-Proliferation of N uclear Weapons (N PT). Yoon later dialed back the statement and assured the media that for now South Korea intends to adhere to its N PT obligations, but his initial statement has already provoked lively discussions on the risks of further nuclear proliferation on the Korean Peninsula.

##### W he re A re We Now?

At present, there is no indication that Seoul is pur- suing a military nuclear program or taking practi- cal steps toward that goal. But there seems to be a critical mass of external and internal factors that are compelling nonproliferation experts to pay closer attention to South Korea’s nuclear policy. First, it is reasonable to assume that Seoul might have incentives to consider further enhancing its military capabilities, including a possible move toward possession of a “super- weapon.” T he security environment on the Korean Peninsula is tense, and a worrying trend toward further escalation has taken form in recent months. N orth Korea continues to develop its nuclear and missile programs — in late December 2022, the leader of N orth Korea (the Democratic People’s R epublic of Korea), Kim Jong Un, announced plans to begin mass production of tactical nuclear weapons and to further build up the country’s nuclear arsenal.

Attempts to reinvigorate regional security dia- logue involving the key stakeholders — the for- mer participants in the Six-Party T alks — have been unsuccessful so far. Against this backdrop, elements of South Korea’s political establish- ment and some experts are increasingly vocal in asking how much reliance can be placed on the US “nuclear umbrella” in the event of an open 4 military conflict in the region. Second, there is no doubt that South Korea has sufficient scientific, technical, industrial and economic capacity. In speaking about the pos- sibility of the country acquiring its own nuclear arsenal, President Yoon emphasized that if the political decision were made, nuclear weapons could be produced in a relatively short amount of time due to already existing expertise. Accord- ing to some sources, representatives from South Korea’s nuclear industry speak about being able to produce nuclear weapons within six months of a political decision to do so. T hird, support for the acquisition of nuclear weapons continues to increase steadily among South Koreans. According to the latest South Korean public opinion polls, three out of four people are in favor of the country possessing its 5 own nuclear weapons. Fourth, South Korea’s nonproliferation record is far from perfect historically. In 1973, South Korea developed a long-term plan to produce nuclear weapons. Incidentally, information about the nuclear activities in South Korea under Presi- dent Park C hung H ee was one of the factors that led to Pyongyang’s sustained interest in pursu- ing its own nuclear program. 6 South Korea’s clan- destine military nuclear program went on for sev- and was finally rolled back in the late 1970s under US pressure. Still, South Korea continued working with sensitive nuclear technology. According to the International Atomic E A),

between 1982 and 2000 the country carried out activities related to uranium conversion, chemi- cal and laser enrichment (including production of highly-enriched uranium) and plutonium sep- aration, all of which were not reported to the IAE A, contrary to South Korea’s obligations under the C omprehensive Safeguards Agreement. It’s 8 worth remembering that in 2002-03 Seoul denied IAE A inspectors the right to access the experimen- tal uranium enrichment facility in South Korea as well as to collect environmental samples. 9 Fur- thermore, in 1997-2001, South Korean agents were known to have attempted to illegally obtain components for intercontinental ballistic missiles capable of carrying nuclear warheads. 10

H owever, Washington has not always been able to prevent its allies from developing nuclear weap- ons — the example of Pakistan comes to mind. South Korea could make a political decision to develop and produce nuclear weapons, either through a clandestine military nuclear program or an open withdrawal from the N PT. A combina- tion of these is also hypothetically possible. In the first case, sooner or later, the IAE A is likely to find indications that Seoul is not comply- ing with its safeguards agreement. Such indica- tions may include anomalies in the accounting data of the reported nuclear material or detec- tion of undeclared nuclear material, facilities and activities. T his would be followed by an IAE A investigation and a report to the IAE A Board of Governors.
